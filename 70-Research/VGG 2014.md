# VGG 2014

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** Instead of a few big magnifying glasses, stack many tiny 3-by-3 ones, one after another. Stacking them lets the network look at the same area and think about it more times, and going deeper turned out to make it more accurate.

Paper: "Very Deep Convolutional Networks for Large-Scale Image Recognition", arXiv 1409.1556, version 6 of 10 April 2015 (the ICLR 2015 camera-ready), 14 pages. Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my own arithmetic, done in Python. This paper's arXiv licence is not open (the usual arXiv licence), so its own tables and pictures are not copied; the one figure is a free redrawing.

## The problem
After [[LeNet and AlexNet|AlexNet]] won ImageNet in 2012, many people tried to improve it: a smaller first filter and a smaller stride in the first layer (ILSVRC-2013 winners), or running the network densely over the whole image at several scales (paper, 1). The authors "address another important aspect of ConvNet architecture design: its depth". They keep everything else fixed and only add layers, so the effect of depth can be measured on its own (paper, 1).

## What people did before
- **AlexNet (2012):** 8 weight layers (5 convolutional and 3 fully connected), with a large 11 by 11 filter and stride 4 in the first layer (paper, 2.3; the layer count is general knowledge). See [[AlexNet 2012]].
- **ZFNet (Zeiler and Fergus, 2013) and OverFeat (Sermanet et al., 2014):** a 7 by 7 filter with stride 2 in the first layer (paper, 2.3). OverFeat has 144 million weights (paper, 2.2).
- **Small filters in shallow nets:** Ciresan et al. (2011) used small filters but nets "significantly less deep", and not on the big ImageNet data (paper, 2.3). Goodfellow et al. (2014) used 11 weight layers for street-number reading and saw depth help (paper, 2.3).
- **[[GoogLeNet 2014]]** (22 weight layers) was developed at the same time and also uses very deep ConvNets, but with a more complex topology (paper, 2.3).

## The idea
Use one very simple building block, a 3 by 3 [[Convolution]] with stride 1 and padding 1, over and over, with a 2 by 2 max pooling now and then, and make the stack deeper in steps: 11, 13, 16, 16 and 19 weight layers (paper, 2.2). Why 3 by 3? Two 3 by 3 layers in a row see a 5 by 5 area, three see a 7 by 7 area (paper, 2.3), so a stack can see as much as one big filter, but it has more [[ReLU]] steps in between and fewer weights. The paper's claim is that depth matters, and that a plain [[Convolutional Neural Network]] with "substantially increased depth" is enough to reach the best results of the time (paper, 5).

## The architecture, part by part

![[vgg16-architecture.png]]

Figure: Gorlapraveen, from [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:VGG16.png), CC BY-SA 4.0. It is a redrawing of the idea, not a figure of the paper (the paper has only Table 1, no picture of the layout). It matches the paper's configuration D (2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 13 convolutions, plus 3 dense = 16 weight layers). One detail differs: the labels in the drawing's fourth block read "4-1, 4-2, 4-2" where "4-3" is meant, and the drawing does not show the number of channels.*

The whole design (paper, 2.1). The input is a fixed 224 by 224 RGB image; the only preprocessing is subtracting the mean RGB value of the training set from each pixel. A stack of convolutions is followed by 3 fully connected layers (4096, 4096 and 1000 channels), then the softmax. Every hidden layer uses ReLU. The 6 configurations A to E differ only in how many convolutions are in the stack.

What each column is, and why it matters:
- **Names A to E:** the configurations, which "differ only in the depth" (paper, 2.2). Only A-LRN differs in kind: it adds local response normalisation, the kind of [[Normalization]] used in AlexNet.
- **conv3 and conv1:** a 3 by 3 or a 1 by 1 convolution. A 1 by 1 convolution mixes the channels at each position and does not look at neighbours (paper, 2.3).
- **Weight layers:** convolutions plus the 3 dense layers, so 8 + 3 = 11 up to 16 + 3 = 19. Pooling layers have no weights and are not counted.
- **Parameters:** the number of weights to learn, in millions. The paper's point is that its deepest net (144 M) has no more weights than the shallower OverFeat (144 M, paper 2.2).

**Summary: what the table shows.**
- **Depth costs few weights, because the dense layers dominate.** Going from A to E adds 8 convolutions and 11 million weights (132.9 M to 143.7 M, mine) while the three dense layers hold 123.6 M in every net (mine). In D the dense layers are 89.4% of all weights (mine).
- **Most of the weights are in one layer.** The first dense layer takes the 7 by 7 by 512 map (25,088 numbers) to 4096, which is 102.8 M weights (mine).
- **Caution:** the table counts weights, not work. Convolutions run at 224 by 224 positions in the first block, so the compute sits in the early convolutions and the memory for the weights sits in the dense layers (my reading).

### Part by part
- **The convolution stack: 3 by 3, stride 1, padding 1.**
  - *What it does:* every convolution has a 3 by 3 window, moves one pixel at a time, and has 1 pixel of padding, so the width and height do not change (paper, 2.1). 3 by 3 is "the smallest size to capture the notion of left/right, up/down, center" (paper, 2.1).
  - *Why it matters:* the feature map only shrinks at the pooling layers, so every size change is a deliberate choice. See [[Convolution]] and [[Feature Map]].
- **Five max-pooling layers, 2 by 2, stride 2.**
  - *What it does:* after some convolutions (not all), a max pooling halves the width and height (paper, 2.1). The map goes 224, 112, 56, 28, 14, 7 (mine, for a 224 input).
  - *Why it matters:* each pooling widens what a later neuron sees and cuts the work. See [[Pooling]].
- **The channel width: 64, then doubled after each pooling, up to 512.**
  - *What it does:* the width is 64, 128, 256, 512, 512 in the five blocks (paper, 2.2). The width is "rather small" and starts at 64 (paper, 2.2).
  - *Why it matters:* width doubles when the map halves, so the work per layer stays about the same. The paper tried only this one width pattern, so it does not say whether wider would help (my reading).
- **The three dense layers: 4096, 4096, 1000.**
  - *What they do:* the last convolution output (7 by 7 by 512) is turned into a vector of 4096, then another 4096, then 1000 class scores, followed by the [[Softmax]]. The same in all six nets (paper, 2.1).
  - *Why they matter:* they hold most of the weights (see the table summary). The paper applies [[Dropout]] with ratio 0.5 to the first two (paper, 3.1).
- **No local response normalisation (except in A-LRN).**
  - *What it does:* nothing. Config A-LRN has LRN and scores no better than A (top-1 29.7 against 29.6, paper's Table 3), so it was dropped from B to E.
  - *Why it matters:* the paper says LRN "leads to increased memory consumption and computation time" with no gain on this data (paper, 2.1).
- **The 1 by 1 convolutions (only in C).**
  - *What they do:* a 1 by 1 convolution is "a linear projection onto the space of the same dimensionality", but the ReLU after it adds a non-linearity, without changing the receptive field (paper, 2.3).
  - *Why they matter:* config C tests whether extra non-linearity alone helps. It does a little (C is better than B), but a 3 by 3 in the same place is better (D beats C), so spatial context matters too (paper, 4.1).

### Why a stack of 3 by 3 and not one big filter (paper, 2.3)

Two things are gained by replacing one 7 by 7 convolution with three 3 by 3 convolutions that see the same 7 by 7 area. First, three ReLU steps instead of one, "which makes the decision function more discriminative". Second, fewer weights: with C channels in and out, three 3 by 3 layers have $3\times(3^{2}C^{2})=27C^{2}$ weights and one 7 by 7 layer has $7^{2}C^{2}=49C^{2}$, "i.e. 81% more" (paper). The paper reads it as a regularisation: the 7 by 7 filter is forced to be built from 3 by 3 filters (paper, 2.3).

**Worked example (tiny numbers, mine).** Take 2 channels in and out (C = 2), ignoring biases.
- Three stacked 3 by 3 convolutions: $27\times4=108$ weights, receptive field $3\to5\to7$.
- One 7 by 7 convolution: $49\times4=196$ weights.
- Ratio $196/108=1.815$, so 81.5% more weights for the single big filter (the paper says 81%).
- At the real widths it is the same ratio: for C = 64, 110,592 against 200,704; for C = 512, 7,077,888 against 12,845,056 (mine).
- Meaning: the same view, nearly half the weights, and two extra ReLU steps for free.

## The core mechanism, step by step

### Training (paper, 3.1)
1. **Start from a fixed-size crop.** A 224 by 224 crop is cut at random from an image whose smallest side has been rescaled to S, then flipped at random and given a random colour shift (paper, 3.1). See [[Data Augmentation]].
2. **Train with mini-batch gradient descent with momentum.** Batch size 256, momentum 0.9, weight decay 5e-4, dropout 0.5 on the first two dense layers, learning rate 0.01 divided by 10 each time the validation accuracy stops improving (3 times in total). Training stopped after 370K iterations (74 epochs) (paper, 3.1). See [[Stochastic Gradient Descent]], [[Momentum]], [[Regularization]] and [[Learning Rate]].
3. **Initialise carefully.** Deep nets are hard to start. They trained the shallow A from random weights, then used A's first four convolutions and last three dense layers to start the deeper ones; the in-between layers started random (normal with variance 0.01). The paper adds that after submission it found that [[Weight Initialization]] from Glorot and Bengio (2010) removes the need for this (paper, 3.1).
4. **Pick the training scale S.** The smallest side of the training image is S. With S = 224 the crop covers the whole image, with S much larger the crop is a small part (an object or an object part). They tried fixed S = 256, fixed S = 384, and a random S in the range 256 to 512 ("scale jittering"), which teaches one net to recognise objects at many sizes (paper, 3.1).

**Worked example (tiny numbers, mine).** A 1.3 M-image training set with batch 256 gives $1{,}300{,}000/256\approx5{,}078$ iterations per epoch, so 74 epochs would be about 375,800 iterations (the paper says 370K, so the numbers agree to within 2%). Four GPUs gave a speed-up of 3.75 times, which is $3.75/4=93.8\%$ of perfect (paper, 3.3, my division); training one net took "2 to 3 weeks" on four Titan Black GPUs.

### Testing: dense evaluation (paper, 3.2)
1. **Rescale** the test image so the smallest side is Q (the test scale; Q need not equal S).
2. **Turn the dense layers into convolutions.** The first becomes a 7 by 7 convolution, the last two become 1 by 1 convolutions.
3. **Run the whole net over the uncropped image.** The result is a class score map, not a single vector, and its size depends on the image size.
4. **Average the map** over space to get one vector of class scores, then also do the same on the flipped image and average the two.
5. Optionally also use multiple crops (50 per scale: a 5 by 5 grid with 2 flips, 150 over 3 scales) and average with the dense result (paper, 3.2).

**Worked example (tiny numbers, mine).** The first dense layer sees 7 by 7 by 512 = 25,088 numbers and has 4096 outputs, so $25{,}088\times4096=102.76$ M weights. As a 7 by 7 convolution with 512 channels in and 4096 out it has $7\times7\times512\times4096=102.76$ M weights as well: the same weights, just arranged as a filter that can slide. On a 224 input the filter fits once (a 1 by 1 output map, the classic result); on a bigger image it slides and gives a bigger score map.

## The equations
The paper has no numbered equation, and none of its ideas is a formula you must write to reproduce it: the training objective is the "multinomial logistic regression objective" (the ordinary [[Cross-Entropy Loss]] with a [[Softmax]], paper, 3.1), and the other rules are arithmetic, explained in sentences above (the 27 against 49 weights; the rule that two 3 by 3 layers see 5 by 5). Under the vault's equation policy, an equation here would be forced, so there is none.

For the localisation experiments (Appendix A) the paper replaces that objective with a Euclidean loss on the box position, which penalises "the deviation of the predicted bounding box parameters from the ground-truth" (paper, A.1), again without writing a formula.

## What they found
The headline numbers (paper): the deepest net reaches 7.0% top-5 error with one network on the ILSVRC test set, 6.8% with two combined (and 7.3% with the 7-network ensemble submitted to the contest); the team was 2nd in classification and 1st in localisation (25.3% error) at ILSVRC-2014 (abstract; paper, 4.5 and A.2). Depth helps up to 19 layers, and the models also work as feature extractors for other data sets (abstract; Appendix B).

### Other tasks (paper's Appendix A and B, tables 8 to 12)
- **Localisation (Appendix A, Tables 8 to 10):** the last layer predicts a 4-number box (centre, width, height). Predicting a separate box per class ("PCR") beats a single class-independent box ("SCR"), 34.3 against 36.4 localisation error with the true class given, and fine-tuning all layers (33.1) beats fine-tuning only the dense layers (34.3). The best fused entry had 25.3% test error and won the ILSVRC-2014 localisation contest, ahead of GoogLeNet (26.7), OverFeat (29.9) and AlexNet's team (34.2) (paper, Table 10). A box counts as correct when its overlap with the true box, [[Intersection over Union]], is above 0.5.
- **Feature extractor (Appendix B, Tables 11 and 12):** drop the last layer, use the 4096-number vector of the one before as an image descriptor, and train only a linear SVM on it, with no fine-tuning. Mean average precision on VOC-2007 was 89.3 (D and E), against 82.4 for the best earlier method (a 6.9 point lead, mine); Caltech-256 85.0 against 77.6; and VOC-2012 action classification 84.0 with image and box, against 76.3 (paper, Tables 11 and 12). It is not a win everywhere: on Caltech-101 VGG's 92.7 trails He et al.'s 93.4. See [[Transfer Learning]].

## Questions I had, answered

**Does the paper show that more depth always helps?** No. It shows that depth helps from 11 to 16 layers on ImageNet, and that 19 layers (E) is no better than 16 (D) on the classification error (27.3 against 27.0 top-1 at S = Q = 256), but better on Caltech. The paper expects "even deeper models might be beneficial for larger datasets" (paper, 4.1) and did not train them.

**Is a 3 by 3 filter stack really the same as a 7 by 7 filter?** It sees the same area (7 by 7), but it is not the same function: the stack can only build a 7 by 7 pattern out of 3 by 3 parts, with ReLU in between. That is why the paper calls it a regularisation of the big filter (paper, 2.3). In my example a stack has 108 weights and a single 7 by 7 filter 196, so the big filter can learn patterns the stack cannot, but the paper's data say the stack wins in accuracy.

**Why does the 1 by 1 convolution in config C help at all if it looks at nothing around it?** It still mixes the channels at each position, and the ReLU after it is an extra non-linear step, which "makes the decision function more discriminative" (paper, 2.3). The effect is small: C is 0.6 points better than B in top-1 (28.1 against 28.7), and D, with a real 3 by 3 there, is another 1.1 better (27.0), mine.

**Why is the test done on whole images when the net was trained on 224 by 224 crops?** Because the dense layers were turned into convolutions, the same weights can slide over a larger image and give a map of scores. This is cheaper than running the net on many crops, which re-computes the overlapping parts (paper, 3.2).

**Why scale jittering?** Objects in photos have very different sizes. If the training image is always rescaled to one size, the network sees objects at one size. Random S in 256 to 512 shows each object at many sizes during training, so one net handles all of them (paper, 3.1). The table agrees: D improves from 27.0 to 25.6 top-1 (mine).

## Why it mattered
It made the lesson plain: a very simple, repeated design, with depth as the only change, was a major lever for accuracy, and it was easy to copy. The authors released their two best models (D and E), and the paper itself reports that other groups soon used them: Girshick et al. replaced the AlexNet network with the 16-layer one to get better object detection (Appendix B, which is the step from [[R-CNN 2013]]), and other work used it for segmentation and captioning (paper, Appendix B). From my background knowledge (not from the paper), VGG-16 became one of the most reused backbones for transfer learning, and [[FCN 2014]] and [[Faster R-CNN 2015]] both used it. Its weakness, the 138 M weights, was fixed by later designs such as [[ResNet 2015]] with far fewer weights.

## Limits and open questions
- **Large:** 138 M weights for D and about 553 MB as 4-byte numbers (mine). Most weights are in one dense layer, 102.8 M (mine).
- **Slow to train:** 2 to 3 weeks on four GPUs for one net (paper, 3.3), and 224 by 224 convolutions with 64 channels in the first block are costly. The paper gives no timing for testing.
- **The training start is hard.** Deeper nets needed to be started from a shallow trained net, which the authors later found unnecessary with a better initialisation (paper, 3.1).
- **Depth saturates at 19 layers on ImageNet** (paper, 4.1); the paper did not try to train deeper nets, and plain nets deeper than that become hard to train, which [[ResNet 2015]] addresses (general knowledge).
- **No ablation of width,** pooling positions or the dense layers: only depth was varied (my reading).
- **Single runs,** no error bars (my reading).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision VGG (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2014, arXiv 1409.1556): what question did it ask?::How much does the depth of a convolutional network matter for accuracy? The authors keep everything else fixed and only add layers (paper, 1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What did people do before VGG to improve AlexNet?::They used a smaller first filter and stride (ZFNet, OverFeat: 7 by 7 stride 2 against AlexNet's 11 by 11 stride 4), and ran the net densely at several scales. VGG instead changed the depth (paper, 1 and 2.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision VGG: what is the idea?::Use only 3 by 3 convolutions (stride 1, padding 1), with 2 by 2 max pooling now and then, and make the stack deeper in steps (11, 13, 16, 16 and 19 weight layers). Stacked small filters see a big area with fewer weights and more ReLU steps.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What are the main ingredients of every VGG network?::A fixed 224 by 224 RGB input (mean subtracted), a stack of 3 by 3 convolutions with stride 1 and padding 1, five 2 by 2 max poolings with stride 2, then three dense layers (4096, 4096, 1000), softmax, and ReLU everywhere (paper, 2.1).

What does this picture show, and where does it differ from the paper?

The 16-layer VGG (configuration D): five blocks of 2, 2, 3, 3, 3 convolutions, each block ending in a pooling layer, then three dense layers. #flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How do the six VGG configurations A to E differ?::Only in depth: 8, 8, 10, 13, 13, 16 convolutions (plus 3 dense layers each) for 11, 11, 13, 16, 16, 19 weight layers. A-LRN adds a normalisation layer, C adds three 1 by 1 convolutions, D uses 3 by 3 throughout, E adds a fourth 3 by 3 in blocks 3 to 5 (paper's Table 1; the counts are mine).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How do width and map size change through VGG?::Width 64, 128, 256, 512, 512 (doubled after each pooling until 512); the map goes 224, 112, 56, 28, 14, 7 at the pooling layers (the width is from the paper, the sizes are my arithmetic for a 224 input).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What is the receptive field of a stack of 3 by 3 convolutions?::Two stacked 3 by 3 layers see 5 by 5, three see 7 by 7 (no pooling in between, stride 1) (paper, 2.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why use three 3 by 3 convolutions instead of one 7 by 7?::The same 7 by 7 view, but with three ReLU steps instead of one (more discriminative) and fewer weights: $27C^{2}$ against $49C^{2}$, so the single filter has 81% more. It also acts like a regularisation of the 7 by 7 filter (paper, 2.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Example: 2 channels in and out. How many weights do three stacked 3 by 3 convolutions and one 7 by 7 convolution have?
?
$3\times9\times4=108$ against $49\times4=196$ (ignoring biases), so the 7 by 7 has 81.5% more weights (my arithmetic; the paper says 81%). At 512 channels it is 7,077,888 against 12,845,056.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What does a 1 by 1 convolution do in config C, and what did the paper find?::It mixes the channels at each position without changing the receptive field, and the ReLU after it adds a non-linear step. C beats B (top-1 28.1 against 28.7) but loses to D, which has 3 by 3 filters at the same positions (27.0), so spatial context matters too (paper, 2.3 and 4.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why did VGG drop local response normalisation?::A-LRN scored no better than A (top-1 29.7 against 29.6) and it "leads to increased memory consumption and computation time" (paper, 2.1 and 4.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Where are most of VGG's weights?::In the three dense layers: 123.6 M of D's 138.4 M (89.4%), and 102.8 M in the first one alone, which takes the 7 by 7 by 512 map (25,088 numbers) to 4096 (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How many weights does VGG D have, and how does it compare with its rivals?::138 M in the paper, 138.4 M in my count; E has 144 M. OverFeat, a shallower net with bigger filters, already had 144 M, so the deeper VGG was not bigger (paper, 2.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What training setup did VGG use?::Mini-batch gradient descent with momentum 0.9, batch 256, weight decay 5e-4, dropout 0.5 on the first two dense layers, learning rate 0.01 divided by 10 three times when validation stalled, 370K iterations (74 epochs) (paper, 3.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How did VGG start training the deep nets, and what did the authors say afterwards?::They trained the shallow net A from random weights and used it to start the first four convolutions and last three dense layers of the deeper nets. After submission they found that Glorot and Bengio's random initialisation works without pre-training (paper, 3.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What is scale jittering in VGG, and why use it?::Each training image is rescaled so its smallest side S is random in 256 to 512, and then a 224 crop is taken. It teaches one net to recognise objects of many sizes. With one test scale, D improved from 27.0 to 25.6 top-1 (my difference, paper's Table 3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How does VGG classify a whole image at test time?::It turns the dense layers into convolutions (7 by 7 then 1 by 1), slides the net over the uncropped image to get a map of class scores, averages it over space, and averages with the flipped image. This avoids re-computing many crops (paper, 3.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Example: why does the first dense layer of VGG have the same weights as a 7 by 7 convolution?
?
The dense layer takes $7\times7\times512=25{,}088$ numbers to 4096: $25{,}088\times4096=102.76$ M weights. A 7 by 7 convolution with 512 channels in and 4096 out has $7\times7\times512\times4096=102.76$ M weights, the same ones arranged as a filter that can slide (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What did the VGG paper conclude about depth in Table 3 (best, worst, caution)?::Top-5 at S = Q = 256 falls from 10.4 (A) to 8.8 (D) and slightly rises to 9.0 (E), so depth helps then saturates at 19 layers; A-LRN is worst-equal (10.5). Jittering helps more (D 8.8 to 8.1). Single runs, so 0.1 differences are noise (my reading).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Which is better in VGG's Table 3, C or D, and what does it show?::D (3 by 3 everywhere) beats C (with three 1 by 1 filters) at the same depth: top-5 8.8 against 9.4. Extra non-linearity helps (C beats B at 9.9) but looking at neighbours helps more.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What was VGG's best single-net and ensemble result on ILSVRC (paper's Tables 5 to 7)?::Best single net E: 7.1% top-5 on validation and 7.0% on test (dense and multi-crop). Best overall: two nets D and E, 6.8% on both; the contest entry of 7 nets scored 7.3% test, and the 6.8% result came after the deadline.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How did VGG compare with GoogLeNet and AlexNet in Table 7?::Single net: VGG 7.0% test against GoogLeNet 7.9%, 0.9 points better. Overall GoogLeNet's 7 nets (6.7) were a hair ahead of VGG's 2 nets (6.8). AlexNet's 5 nets had 16.4%, so VGG's contest entry (7.3) cut the error by 55.5% (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What does multi-crop evaluation do, and is it worth it?::It averages predictions of 50 crops per scale, 150 over 3 scales, and slightly beats dense evaluation (top-5 7.4 against 7.5 for E); the two together are best (7.1). The authors say the extra time "does not justify" the gain (paper, 3.2 and 4.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How did VGG handle localisation, and what did it win?::The last layer predicts a 4-number box (centre, width, height); a per-class box (PCR) beat a shared box (SCR), 34.3 against 36.4, and fine-tuning all layers beat fine-tuning only the dense ones (33.1). The fused entry had 25.3% test error and won the ILSVRC-2014 localisation track (paper, Appendix A).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How did VGG do as a plain feature extractor (Appendix B)?::Drop the last layer, use the 4096-number vector as a descriptor with a linear SVM and no fine-tuning: 89.3 mAP on VOC-2007 against 82.4 for the best earlier method (my difference 6.9), Caltech-256 85.0 against 77.6. Not a win everywhere: on Caltech-101 He et al.'s 93.4 beat VGG's 92.7.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Is 19 layers always better than 16 in VGG?::No. On ImageNet classification E (19 layers) is no better than D (16): top-1 27.3 against 27.0 at S = Q = 256. On Caltech it is a little better. The paper only says deeper "might be beneficial for larger datasets" and did not train deeper (paper, 4.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why did the paper turn the dense layers into convolutions for testing?::So the same weights can slide over an image of any size and give a score map, instead of running the net on many crops that re-compute overlapping parts (paper, 3.2).
