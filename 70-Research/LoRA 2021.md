# LoRA 2021

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** Instead of rewriting every number inside a giant model to teach it something new, keep the giant model frozen and add a tiny "correction sheet" next to each big grid of numbers. Only the small sheets are trained, and they are so small that you can keep one per task.

Paper: "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models", arXiv 2106.09685. Version 1 was posted on 17 June 2021 (arXiv listing), and I read version 2 of 16 October 2021 (paper's first page). Concept note: [[LoRA]].

## The problem
Large language models are pre-trained once and then adapted to many tasks. Full [[Fine-tuning]] retrains **all** parameters, so every task needs its own full copy of the model. The abstract's example: deploying independent fine-tuned instances of GPT-3 175B, each with 175 billion parameters, is prohibitively expensive (abstract, paper).

## What people did before
- **Full fine-tuning:** simple and strong, but a new full-size model per task, with the memory cost of pre-training (paper).
- **Adapter layers** (Houlsby et al., 2019 and later variants): small extra layers inserted into each Transformer block. The paper argues they add **inference latency**, because they must run one after another even though they are small, and it shows this on GPT-2 medium (see below) (paper).
- **Prompt and prefix tuning** (Li and Liang, 2021 and others): train special input tokens or activations instead. The paper says prefix tuning is hard to optimise, its performance changes non-monotonically with the number of trainable parameters, and the reserved tokens eat into the sequence length available for the task (paper). See [[Prompt Engineering]] for the plain-text version of the idea.
- **Bias-only training (BitFit):** train only the bias vectors, as a cheap baseline (paper).
- The paper takes inspiration from work (Li et al., 2018; Aghajanyan et al., 2020) showing that over-parametrised models have a low "intrinsic dimension" (paper).

## The idea
Keep the giant pre-trained model frozen and teach it a new task by training only a tiny extra pair of matrices next to each chosen big matrix. The authors' bet is that the *change* a task needs is simple (has a "low intrinsic rank"), even though the model itself is huge, so a very thin pair is enough (paper). After training, the pair is merged into the big matrix, so the finished model is as fast as an ordinary fine-tuned one.

### What LoRA is: full fine-tuning and LoRA side by side
LoRA is a way of **fine-tuning a pre-trained model**: it adapts a model that already knows language to your task. It is not pre-training and not a new kind of model; it only changes *how you teach the model a new task*. Marks: "(paper)" read in the paper; "(mine)" my arithmetic.

| | Full fine-tuning | LoRA |
|---|---|---|
| Starts from | the pre-trained model | the same pre-trained model |
| What can change | every weight | nothing in the original weights (frozen); a small extra pair of matrices $A$ and $B$ next to chosen matrices is trained |
| Trained values on GPT-3 175B | 175,255.8 million (paper, Table 4) | 4.7 million or 37.7 million (paper, Table 4); 18.9 million at $r=4$ on $W_q$ and $W_v$, about $0.011\%$ of the model (mine) |
| What you save per task | the whole model, about 350 GB (paper) | only $A$ and $B$, about 35 MB (paper), roughly 10,000 times smaller |
| GPU memory to train GPT-3 175B | 1.2 TB (paper) | 350 GB (paper) |
| Extra delay when serving | none | none, because $A$ and $B$ are merged into the big matrices after training (adapter layers, another method, do add delay) |
| Quality in the paper's tests | the baseline | on par or better on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2 and GPT-3 (abstract); not tested on other kinds of networks |

*A table written by me from the numbers in this note; the first two rows of numbers come from the paper's Table 4 and section 4.2.*

## The architecture (figures)
LoRA does not change the shape of the model. It takes a few of the model's big weight matrices, freezes them, and puts a small trainable pair of matrices **beside** each one. The pair's output is added to the big matrix's output (paper, Figure 1 and 4.1). In the experiments the matrices chosen are the attention query ($W_q$) and value ($W_v$) matrices of every layer (paper, 4.2). Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my arithmetic.

The paper's Figure 1 ("Our reparametrization. We only train $A$ and $B$.") shows one weight matrix: the input $x$ goes into two parallel paths, the frozen pre-trained weights $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and, beside them, $A$ (shrinks $x$ from $d$ numbers to $r$ numbers) followed by $B$ (grows it back to $d$); the two outputs are added to give $h$.

### Part by part
- **The frozen pre-trained weights $W_0$.**
  - *What it does:* the original weight matrix ($d\times k$) is kept exactly as it was and receives no updates (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* frozen weights need no gradients and no optimizer state, so with Adam the training memory falls by up to two thirds, and training was about 25% faster on GPT-3 175B (paper, 4.2). One big frozen base model can serve many tasks.
- **Matrix $A$ ($r\times k$): the shrinking half.**
  - *What it does:* turns the input's $k$ numbers into only $r$ numbers. It starts as random Gaussian numbers (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* $r$ numbers is the narrow neck through which the whole update must pass. The random start makes sure $Ax$ is not zero, so $B$ receives a gradient straight away (the gradient reasoning is mine).
- **Matrix $B$ ($d\times r$): the growing half.**
  - *What it does:* turns those $r$ numbers back into $d$ numbers, the size of the layer's output. It starts at zero (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* because $B=0$, the product $BA=0$ and the model starts exactly as the pre-trained model, so training begins from a good place.
- **The rank $r$.**
  - *What it does:* the inner size of $BA$, which limits the update to $r$ directions. It is much smaller than $d$ and $k$ (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* the authors hypothesise that the weight change during adaptation has a low "intrinsic rank". Evidence (paper, Table 6, GPT-3): with $W_q$ and $W_v$ adapted, WikiSQL accuracy was 73.4 at $r=1$ and 73.8 at $r=8$, flat within the noise. A larger $r$ costs more parameters and, in that table, did not help. The authors do not expect a small $r$ to work for every task, for example a new language.
- **The scale $\alpha/r$.**
  - *What it does:* the update $BAx$ is multiplied by $\alpha/r$, where $\alpha$ is a constant. The authors set $\alpha$ to the first $r$ they try and do not tune it (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* it "helps to reduce the need to retune hyperparameters when we vary $r$" (paper). My reasoning: $BAx$ sums $r$ terms, so a bigger $r$ makes it bigger, and dividing by $r$ keeps its size about the same.
- **Which matrices get a pair: $W_q$ and $W_v$.**
  - *What it does:* a Transformer layer has four attention matrices ($W_q$, $W_k$, $W_v$, $W_o$) and two in the feed-forward part. LoRA is applied only to $W_q$ and $W_v$; the feed-forward layers, LayerNorm and biases stay frozen, "for simplicity and parameter-efficiency" (paper, 4.2).
  - *Why it matters:* with the same budget (about 18M trained values), the paper's Table 5 shows spreading it over $W_q$ and $W_v$ at rank 4 (73.7 on WikiSQL) beats putting it all into $W_q$ (70.4) or $W_k$ (70.0). Adapting all four at rank 2 ties it on WikiSQL (73.7) and is higher on MultiNLI (91.7 against 91.3), so the choice of $W_q$ with $W_v$ is not clearly the best. The paper leaves the feed-forward layers, LayerNorm and biases to future work.
- **The addition of the two paths.**
  - *What it does:* $h=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAx$: both paths see the same input $x$ and their outputs are added coordinate by coordinate (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* both paths are linear, so they can be merged into one matrix after training (next part).
- **Merging for serving, and switching tasks.**
  - *What it does:* after training, compute and store $W=W_0+BA$ and run the model as usual. To switch to another task, subtract $BA$ and add a different $B'A'$ (paper, 4.1).
  - *Why it matters:* the merged model is the same size and takes the same time as a fine-tuned one, so "we do not introduce any additional latency during inference" (paper). The limit: if you merge, it is not straightforward to put inputs for different tasks in one batch (paper, 4.2).

### Size check (my arithmetic, GPT-3 175B sizes)
GPT-3 175B has width $d_{\text{model}}=12{,}288$ and 96 layers (paper, 1 and 7.1). LoRA at $r=4$ on $W_q$ and $W_v$ is $2\times192\times12{,}288\times4=18{,}874{,}368$ trained values; stored as 16-bit numbers (2 bytes each) that is about $37.7$ million bytes (the paper says roughly 35 MB). The full model at 2 bytes per number is $175\times10^9\times2=350$ GB, which matches the paper's 350 GB, so the checkpoint is about 10,000 times smaller. For 100 tasks the paper's count is $350\text{ GB}+100\times35\text{ MB}\approx354$ GB instead of about 35 TB.

## The core mechanism, step by step

### One LoRA layer in training
**The idea (my analogy).** Think of the big weight matrix as a printed textbook that must not be changed. LoRA attaches a thin sheet of sticky notes to it. The sheet is small, but when you read, you read the book and the notes together.

1. **The input $x$ enters both paths.**
   - *What it does:* $x$ goes through the frozen $W_0$ and, separately, through the pair $A$ then $B$ (paper, Figure 1).
   - *Why it matters:* the frozen path gives the pre-trained answer $W_0x$ untouched.
2. **$A$ shrinks it: $Ax$.**
   - *What it does:* multiplies $x$ ($k$ numbers) by $A$ ($r\times k$) to get $r$ numbers.
   - *Why it matters:* this is the narrow neck that limits the update to $r$ directions.
3. **$B$ grows it back: $BAx$.**
   - *What it does:* multiplies those $r$ numbers by $B$ ($d\times r$) to get $d$ numbers.
   - *Why it matters:* the result has the same size as $W_0x$, so the two can be added.
4. **Scale by $\alpha/r$ and add: $h=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAx$.**
   - *What it does:* the small correction is scaled and added to the frozen output.
   - *Why it matters:* at the start $B=0$, so $h=W_0x$; training only has to learn the correction.
5. **Backward: only $A$ and $B$ are updated.**
   - *What it does:* the error is passed back; gradients are computed for $A$ and $B$ and they are updated, while $W_0$ gets no update (paper, 4.1). The error still has to flow *through* $W_0$ to reach earlier layers' $A$ and $B$ (general knowledge).
   - *Why it matters:* there are only $r(d+k)$ numbers to update and store optimizer state for, instead of $dk$.

**Worked example (mine; $d=2$, $k=3$, $r=1$, $\alpha=2$, so $\alpha/r=2$).** $W_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $x=(1,2,3)$, $A=(1,0,-1)$, and at the start $B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}$.
- Forward: $Ax=-2$; $BAx=(0,0)$; $h=W_0x=(1,2)$. The model gives the pre-trained answer.
- Suppose the error at the output is $g=(1,\,0.5)$. The gradient for $B$ is $\frac{\alpha}{r}\,g\,(Ax)=2\cdot(1,0.5)\cdot(-2)=(-4,\,-2)$, not zero, because $Ax=-2$. The gradient for $A$ is $0$, because $B=0$.
- One plain gradient step with learning rate $0.1$ (my illustration; the paper uses Adam) gives $B=(0.4,\,0.2)$.
- Now $A$'s gradient is no longer zero: $2\cdot(0.4\cdot1+0.2\cdot0.5)\cdot x=(1,2,3)$. So both matrices start learning after the first step. If $A$ had also started at zero, $Ax$ would be $0$, $B$'s gradient would be $0$, and nothing would ever move.

**Shapes for $W_q$ of GPT-3 175B at $r=4$ (mine).**

|---|---|---|
| $W_0$ (frozen) | $12288\times12288$ | $150{,}994{,}944$ |
| $A$ (trained) | $4\times12288$ | $49{,}152$ |
| $B$ (trained) | $12288\times4$ | $49{,}152$ |

### Serving: merge and switch tasks
1. **Train $A$ and $B$ for a task.** The 175-billion-parameter base model is shared by all tasks.
2. **Merge: $W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$.** *What it does:* one ordinary matrix replaces the two paths. *Why it matters:* it reproduces the same outputs with no extra delay. My check with the example above: $W=W_0+2BA$ gives $Wx=(-3,-6)$, the same as the two-path result.
3. **Run the model as usual.** It has exactly the shape of the original, so any serving software works (general knowledge).
4. **Switch task: subtract $\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ and add $\frac{\alpha}{r}B'A'$.** *What it does:* recovers $W_0$ and applies another task's update. *Why it matters:* a quick operation with very little memory overhead (paper, 4.1); switching means swapping about 35 MB, not 350 GB.
5. **Limit.** Once merged, inputs for different tasks cannot easily share one batch; not merging and picking the module per example is possible when latency is not critical (paper, 4.2).

## The equations (paper, sections 4.1, 5.1 and 7.2)

**1. Low-rank update** (section 4.1)

$$
W=W_0+\Delta W=W_0+BA,\qquad B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\ r\ll\min(d,k)
$$

**Where:**
- $W_0$: the frozen pre-trained weight matrix, $d\times k$.
- $\Delta W$: the change learned for the new task; it is never stored as a full $d\times k$ matrix.
- $B$, $A$: the two thin trained matrices; $r$ is the rank (their shared inner size).

**Matters:** $BA$ can have rank at most $r$, so it can only change the weights in $r$ directions. That limit is the whole saving: far fewer numbers to train, store and keep optimizer state for. Without the low-rank constraint this is ordinary fine-tuning.

**Example (tiny numbers):** $d=2$, $k=3$, $r=1$, with $B=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$ and $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
- $BA=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\2&0&-2\end{pmatrix}$, a $2\times3$ matrix built from only $2+3=5$ trained numbers instead of $6$.
- The second row is exactly twice the first, which is what rank 1 means.
- At the paper's scale a single $12{,}288\times12{,}288$ matrix has $150{,}994{,}944$ entries, and a rank-4 update has $12{,}288\cdot4+4\cdot12{,}288=98{,}304$, about $1{,}536$ times fewer (my arithmetic; $d=12{,}288$ is stated in the paper's introduction).

**2. The forward pass, with scale** (equation 3 and the scale in section 4.1)

$$
h=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx
$$

**Where:**
- $x$: the input vector; $h$: the output vector.
- $\alpha$: a constant. The paper scales $\Delta Wx$ by $\alpha/r$ and sets $\alpha$ to the first $r$ it tries, without tuning it. (Its equation 3 is written without the scale; the scale is stated in the text.)
- Both $W_0$ and $BA$ are applied to the same $x$ and their outputs are added.

**Matters:** because $B$ starts at zero, $h=W_0x$ at the first step, so training begins exactly at the pre-trained model. The $\alpha/r$ scale reduces the need to retune the learning rate when $r$ changes (paper, citing Yang and Hu, 2021). The same formula lets you **merge** after training: $W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ gives identical outputs with no extra delay.

**Example (tiny numbers):** $W_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $x=(1,2,3)$, $B$ and $A$ as above, $r=1$, $\alpha=2$ (so $\alpha/r=2$).
- $Ax=1\cdot1+0\cdot2+(-1)\cdot3=-2$; $BAx=(-2,\,-4)$; scaled by 2: $(-4,\,-8)$.
- $W_0x=(1,2)$, so $h=(1-4,\ 2-8)=(-3,\,-6)$.
- Merged check: $W=W_0+2BA=\begin{pmatrix}3&0&-2\\4&1&-4\end{pmatrix}$ and $Wx=(3-6,\ 4+2-12)=(-3,\,-6)$, the same.
- Meaning: the small side path changed the output, and merging reproduces it with one ordinary matrix.

**3. Number of trained parameters** (section 5.1)

$$
|\Theta|=2\times\hat L_{\text{LoRA}}\times d_{\text{model}}\times r
$$

**Where:**
- $\hat L_{\text{LoRA}}$: how many weight matrices get a LoRA pair.
- $d_{\text{model}}$: the width of the model ($12{,}288$ for GPT-3 175B).
- $r$: the rank. The factor 2 is for the two thin matrices $A$ and $B$ of each pair (each $d_{\text{model}}\times r$ for square matrices).

**Matters:** the count grows only linearly with $r$ and not with $d_{\text{model}}^2$, which is why it is tiny next to the model. It also depends on how many matrices you adapt.

**Example (tiny numbers):** GPT-3 175B has 96 layers (the paper says "all 96 layers"); adapting $W_q$ and $W_v$ gives $\hat L=2\times96=192$ matrices.
- $r=4$: $2\times192\times12{,}288\times4=18{,}874{,}368$, about $18.9$ million, the "18M" budget of the paper's section 7.1.
- $r=1$: $4{,}718{,}592$ (4.7M) and $r=8$: $37{,}748{,}736$ (37.7M). These match the 4.7M and 37.7M rows of Table 4. The text I read does not state which rank each row used, so that match is my inference.
- Meaning: 18.9 million against 175 billion is roughly $1/9{,}000$ of the model's numbers (my arithmetic; the paper says "10,000 times" for $r=4$).

**4. Subspace similarity** (equation 4, section 7.2)

$$
\varphi(A_{r=8},A_{r=64},i,j)=\frac{\left\lVert U_{A_{r=8}}^{i\top}\,U_{A_{r=64}}^{j}\right\rVert_F^{2}}{\min(i,j)}\ \in[0,1]
$$

**Where:**
- $A_{r=8}$, $A_{r=64}$: the learned $A$ matrices from two runs with rank 8 and rank 64.
- $U^{i}$: the columns of the right-singular matrix (from an SVD, [[Singular Value Decomposition]]) for the top $i$ singular directions.
- $\lVert\cdot\rVert_F$: the Frobenius norm (square root of the sum of squared entries). Dividing by $\min(i,j)$ makes the result lie between 0 and 1.

**Matters:** 1 means the two sets of directions fully overlap and 0 means they are completely separate. The paper found the top direction overlaps strongly (above 0.5) between rank 8 and rank 64 and the other directions mostly do not, and reads this as "a low-rank adaptation matrix is sufficient" (paper).

**Example (tiny numbers):** two directions in 3 dimensions. $u=(1,0,0)$ and $v=(0.6,0.8,0)$.
- For $i=j=1$: $u^\top v=0.6$; $0.6^2=0.36$; divide by $\min(1,1)=1$: $\varphi=0.36$ (the cosine squared of the angle between them).
- If both sets are $\{e_1,e_2\}$ against $\{e_1,e_3\}$ ($i=j=2$): the product matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, Frobenius squared is 1, divided by 2 gives $0.5$: one of the two directions is shared.

## What they found
Each number is marked "abstract" or "paper" (read in the paper's body text, as extracted from the PDF).

### Headline claims (abstract and paper)
- Compared with GPT-3 175B fine-tuned with Adam, LoRA reduces trainable parameters by **10,000 times** and the GPU memory requirement by **3 times**, and performs on par or better in quality on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2 and GPT-3, with higher training throughput and, unlike adapters, no extra inference latency (abstract).

### Cost on GPT-3 175B (paper, section 4.2)
- Training memory fell from **1.2 TB to 350 GB**. With $r=4$ and only $W_q$ and $W_v$ adapted, the checkpoint shrank from **350 GB to 35 MB**, roughly 10,000 times. Training was about **25% faster** than full fine-tuning: 43.1 against 32.5 tokens per second per V100 GPU. With Adam, VRAM use falls by up to two thirds because the frozen parameters need no optimizer state. The paper adds that storing 100 adapted models then needs about 354 GB, not about 35 TB (paper).
- The number of trained parameters for LoRA on $\hat L$ matrices of width $d_{model}$ is $2\times\hat L\times d_{model}\times r$ (paper).

### Adapter latency (paper, Table 1, GPT-2 medium, one forward pass, batch size 1, sequence length 128)
- Fine-tuning and LoRA: 19.8 ms. AdapterL: 23.9 ms (+20.7%). AdapterH: 25.8 ms (+30.3%). The gap shrinks for larger batches and longer sequences (paper).

**What each column is, and why it matters**
- **Setting:** the method being timed. "Fine-tune or LoRA" is one row because, after training, LoRA's matrices are merged into the original ones, so the model has the same shape and takes the same time as fine-tuning. AdapterL and AdapterH are two adapter designs, which add extra layers.
- **Batch size and sequence length:** how many examples are processed at once and how long each is. A batch of 1 with 128 tokens is like serving one user at a time; 32 by 512 is like bulk work. It matters because the cost of the adapters depends on this.
- **Numbers:** milliseconds for one forward pass, averaged over 100 trials; lower is better. The percentage is the extra time compared with fine-tuning. It matters because adapters add about 20 to 30% delay in the one-user case, but only 2 to 3% for large batches.

The adapter delay matters most for short sequences and small batches (the online case).

**Summary: what the table shows**
- **Fastest:** fine-tuning and LoRA, in all three settings (19.8 ms at batch 1 and length 128).
- **Adapters are slower:** AdapterL +20.7% and AdapterH +30.3% in the one-user setting, but only +2.2% and +3.0% for the large batch of 32 by 512. AdapterH is always slower than AdapterL.
- **So:** the delay matters most for small, one-at-a-time serving, and LoRA has none.

### Quality, per model (paper; "FT" is full fine-tuning; higher is better)
- **RoBERTa base on GLUE (Table 2):** average 86.4 for FT (125.0M trained numbers) against 87.2 for LoRA (0.3M).
- **RoBERTa large:** 88.9 for FT (355.0M) against 89.0 for LoRA (0.8M).
- **DeBERTa XXL:** 91.1 for FT (1,500.0M) against 91.3 for LoRA (4.7M).
- **GPT-2 medium on E2E NLG (Table 3):** BLEU 68.2 for FT (354.92M) against 70.4 for LoRA (0.35M). **GPT-2 large:** 68.5 for FT (774.03M) against 70.4 for LoRA (0.77M).
- **GPT-3 175B (Table 4):** on WikiSQL, MNLI-matched and SAMSum, FT (175,255.8M trained numbers) got 73.8, 89.5 and 52.0/28.0/44.5 (Rouge-1/2/L). LoRA with 4.7M got 73.4, 91.7 and 53.8/29.8/45.9, and LoRA with 37.7M got 74.0, 91.6 and 53.4/29.2/45.1. The paper says LoRA matches or exceeds fine-tuning on all three, and also states the run-to-run noise: about ±0.5% on WikiSQL and ±0.1% on MNLI-m. So the 73.4 against 73.8 on WikiSQL is inside the noise.
- For some of the baselines, the paper reuses numbers published by other authors (marked with an asterisk in its tables), so not every comparison is a controlled rerun (paper).

## Questions I had, answered

**Is LoRA just fine-tuning fewer layers?** No. The paper's baseline "FTTop2" really does that: it trains only the last two layers of GPT-2 medium, which is 25.19M trained values and scored 68.1 BLEU on E2E, against LoRA's 70.4 BLEU with 0.35M values (paper, Table 3). LoRA touches every layer, but only through a small low-rank change.

**Does a low rank limit what the model can learn?** For one matrix, yes, by design: $BA$ can change the weights in only $r$ directions. The authors' view is that adaptation does not need more. Two supports: the paper says that applying LoRA to all weight matrices (and training the biases) and setting $r$ to the rank of the pre-trained matrices "roughly recovers the expressiveness of full fine-tuning" (4.1), and in GPT-3 a rank of 1 already worked on WikiSQL and MNLI (Table 6). The caveat is theirs too: a task in a very different language might need a larger $r$ (paper, footnote in 7.2).

**What happens if I give LoRA more and more trained parameters?** According to the paper, LoRA roughly converges to training the original model, while adapter methods converge to an MLP and prefix methods to a model that cannot take long input sequences (paper, 4.1).

**How is LoRA different from adapter layers?** Adapters are inserted one after another with the model's layers, with a nonlinearity between two small layers, so they add depth and cannot be folded into a matrix; on GPT-2 medium at batch 1 and length 128 they cost +20.7% (AdapterL) and +30.3% (AdapterH) time (paper, Table 1). LoRA's pair sits in parallel and is linear, so it merges and adds no latency.

**How is it different from prompt or prefix tuning?** Those train special input tokens or activations instead of weights. The paper says they reserve part of the sequence length for the task, and that performance is not monotonic in the number of trained parameters: it dropped when using more than 256 special tokens for prefix-embedding tuning or more than 32 for prefix-layer tuning (paper, 5.5 and its Figure 2).

**What does "$\Delta W$ amplifies features" mean?** For layer 48 of GPT-3 at $r=4$, $\lVert W_q\rVert_F=61.95$ and $\lVert\Delta W_q\rVert_F=6.91$. The update correlates with $W$ more than a random matrix would, but it points at directions that $W$ itself does **not** emphasise, and it amplifies them by a large factor, about 21.5 ($6.91/0.32$) (paper, 7.3). The paper's reading: LoRA boosts features that pre-training learned but did not stress.

## Why it mattered
It made adaptation of very large models cheap in memory, storage and serving: one frozen base model plus a small file per task, merged for serving with no extra latency. The paper released a PyTorch package and checkpoints for RoBERTa, DeBERTa and GPT-2 (abstract). From memory (not checked in this paper): the idea became the standard way to fine-tune open language models, it is implemented in libraries such as `peft`, and variants such as QLoRA build on it (see [[QLoRA]]; I did not read that paper here). In my own project, a LoRA layer trained 0.109% of a 0.5-billion-parameter model on a laptop CPU ([[LoRA Fine-tuning on CPU]]).

## Limits and open questions
- **Batching different tasks:** if $A$ and $B$ are merged into $W$ to remove latency, it is not straightforward to batch inputs for different tasks in one pass. Not merging and choosing the module per example is possible when latency is not critical (paper, section 4.2).
- **Small rank is not universal:** the authors do not expect a small $r$ to work for every task. Their thought experiment: if the downstream task were in a different language from the pre-training, retraining the whole model could beat a small-rank LoRA (paper, footnote).
- **Heuristic choices:** the choice of which weight matrices to adapt relies on heuristics, and the authors ask for a more principled way. Adapting MLP layers, LayerNorm and biases was not studied (paper, sections 4.2 and 8).
- **The mechanism is unclear:** the authors say the way features learned in pre-training are transformed for a downstream task is "far from clear", and that LoRA makes it more tractable to study (paper, section 8).
- **Evidence mostly on language models:** the experiments are on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2 and GPT-3. The paper says the principle should apply to any dense layers, but did not test other kinds of networks (paper).
- **Baselines partly reused:** several baseline numbers come from other papers' setups (paper).
- **Open point:** the rank findings are measured on GPT-3 175B. Whether the same tiny ranks suffice for small models and hard tasks is not settled by this paper.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the LoRA low-rank update and explain each symbol.
?
$$W=W_0+\Delta W=W_0+BA,\qquad B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\ r\ll\min(d,k)$$
- $W_0$: frozen pre-trained matrix ($d\times k$); $\Delta W$: the learned change, never stored in full.
- $B$, $A$: the two thin trained matrices; $r$: the rank, which limits $\Delta W$ to $r$ directions and is the whole saving.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm LoRA example: $d=2$, $k=3$, $r=1$, $B=(1,2)^T$, $A=(1,0,-1)$. What is $BA$ and how many numbers are trained?
?
$BA=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\2&0&-2\end{pmatrix}$ (second row is twice the first: rank 1), built from $2+3=5$ trained numbers instead of 6. At the paper's size a $12{,}288\times12{,}288$ matrix has 150,994,944 entries and a rank-4 update 98,304, about 1,536 times fewer (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the LoRA forward pass with its scale and explain each symbol.
?
$$h=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx$$
- $x$: input; $h$: output; $W_0$ frozen; $B$ starts at zero, so $h=W_0x$ at step one.
- $\alpha/r$: the scale; the paper sets $\alpha$ to the first $r$ it tries and does not tune it. It lessens retuning when $r$ changes.
- After training, $W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ gives the same output with no extra delay.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm LoRA forward pass example: $W_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $x=(1,2,3)$, $B=(1,2)^T$, $A=(1,0,-1)$, $r=1$, $\alpha=2$. What is $h$?
?
$Ax=-2$; $BAx=(-2,-4)$; times 2 gives $(-4,-8)$; $W_0x=(1,2)$; so $h=(-3,-6)$. Merged $W=W_0+2BA$ gives $Wx=(-3,-6)$, the same.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the formula for the number of trained parameters in LoRA and explain each symbol.
?
$$|\Theta|=2\times\hat L_{\text{LoRA}}\times d_{\text{model}}\times r$$
- $\hat L_{\text{LoRA}}$: number of weight matrices that get a LoRA pair; $d_{\text{model}}$: model width; $r$: rank.
- The 2 is for $A$ and $B$. The count grows with $r$, not with $d_{\text{model}}^2$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm For GPT-3 175B with $W_q$ and $W_v$ adapted in all 96 layers, how many LoRA parameters at $r=1$, 4 and 8?
?
$\hat L=192$, $d_{\text{model}}=12{,}288$: $r=1$ gives 4,718,592 (4.7M); $r=4$ gives 18,874,368 (about 18.9M, the paper's "18M" budget); $r=8$ gives 37,748,736 (37.7M) (my arithmetic). The 4.7M and 37.7M match the rows of the paper's Table 4, but the text I read does not state which rank each row used.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the paper's subspace similarity and explain each symbol.
?
$$\varphi(A_{r=8},A_{r=64},i,j)=\frac{\left\lVert U_{A_{r=8}}^{i\top}\,U_{A_{r=64}}^{j}\right\rVert_F^{2}}{\min(i,j)}\in[0,1]$$
- $U^i$: top-$i$ right-singular directions (from an SVD) of the learned $A$ of each run; $\lVert\cdot\rVert_F$: Frobenius norm.
- 1 means the subspaces fully overlap, 0 completely separate. The top direction overlapped above 0.5 between rank 8 and 64; the rest mostly did not.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Subspace similarity example: $u=(1,0,0)$, $v=(0.6,0.8,0)$, $i=j=1$. What is $\varphi$?
?
$u^\top v=0.6$; $0.6^2=0.36$; divided by $\min(1,1)=1$ gives $0.36$, the cosine squared of the angle between the two directions. With sets $\{e_1,e_2\}$ and $\{e_1,e_3\}$ ($i=j=2$) it is $1/2=0.5$: one of two directions is shared.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm In LoRA, why two thin matrices $B$ ($d\times r$) and $A$ ($r\times k$) and not one small matrix?
?
The change $\Delta W$ must have the same shape as $W_0$ ($d\times k$) to be added to it. The product $BA$ always has that shape, but it can only have rank up to $r$, and it needs only $r(d+k)$ trained numbers instead of $dk$. My arithmetic for one $12{,}288\times12{,}288$ matrix at $r=4$: $98{,}304$ numbers against $150{,}994{,}944$, about 1,536 times fewer. One small matrix could not be added to a big one.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Why does LoRA require a small rank $r\ll\min(d,k)$, and what is the evidence it works?
?
The paper hypothesises that the weight change during adaptation has a low "intrinsic rank" (inspired by earlier work on low intrinsic dimension), so a very thin $BA$ can capture it. Evidence (Table 6, GPT-3): with $W_q$ and $W_v$ adapted, WikiSQL accuracy was 73.4 at $r=1$ and 73.8 at $r=8$ (73.5 at $r=64$): flat within the noise. Also the top singular direction learned at $r=8$ overlaps strongly with the one at $r=64$. The authors do not expect a small $r$ to work for every task, for example a new language.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Why keep $W_0$ frozen in LoRA?
?
Frozen weights need no gradients and no optimizer state, so with Adam the training memory falls by up to two thirds, and training was about 25% faster on GPT-3 175B (paper, 4.2). Only $A$ and $B$ are saved per task: 35 MB instead of 350 GB with $r=4$ on $W_q$ and $W_v$. One big frozen base model can then serve many tasks by swapping tiny files.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Why does LoRA start with $B=0$ and $A$ random, and not both zero?
?
- **$B=0$:** then $BA=0$, so $h=W_0x$ at the first step: training starts exactly at the pre-trained model (paper).
- **Not both zero (my reasoning from the gradients):** the gradient for $B$ is the output error times $(Ax)^T$, and the gradient for $A$ is $B^T$ times the error. If both were zero, both gradients would be zero and nothing would ever change. With a random $A$, $Ax$ is not zero, so $B$ gets a gradient at once and becomes nonzero after the first step.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Why scale the LoRA update by $\alpha/r$?
?
The paper says the scaling "helps to reduce the need to retune hyperparameters when we vary $r$", and that tuning $\alpha$ is roughly like tuning the learning rate, so it sets $\alpha$ to the first $r$ it tries and does not tune it. My reasoning: $BAx$ adds up $r$ terms, so a bigger $r$ makes the update bigger; dividing by $r$ keeps its size about the same. Example: $\alpha=2$ gives a factor 2 at $r=1$ and 0.25 at $r=8$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Why can LoRA be merged into the model with no extra delay, while adapter layers cannot?
?
LoRA's two paths are both linear and see the same input, so they add up to one matrix: $W_0x+BAx=(W_0+BA)x$. My check: $W=W_0+2BA$ gives $Wx=(-3,-6)$, the same as the two-path result. An adapter has a nonlinearity between its two layers and is inserted in sequence, so it cannot be folded into one matrix and adds depth, which costs time: +20.7% and +30.3% for AdapterL and AdapterH at batch 1, length 128 (paper, Table 1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm In $|\Theta|=2\times\hat L_{\text{LoRA}}\times d_{\text{model}}\times r$, why the factor 2 and why linear in $r$?
?
Each LoRA pair has two matrices, $A$ ($r\times d_{\text{model}}$) and $B$ ($d_{\text{model}}\times r$), each with $d_{\text{model}}\cdot r$ numbers, so $2\,d_{\text{model}}\,r$ per pair, times the number of adapted matrices $\hat L$ (for non-square matrices it is $r(d+k)$). The count grows only linearly with $r$, so doubling the rank doubles the cost: for GPT-3 175B on $W_q$ and $W_v$ it is 4,718,592 at $r=1$ and 18,874,368 at $r=4$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Why does the paper's subspace similarity square the Frobenius norm and divide by $\min(i,j)$?
?
For two sets of orthonormal directions, $\lVert U^{i\top}U^{j}\rVert_F^2$ is the sum of the squared cosines of the angles between the two subspaces, and there are $\min(i,j)$ such angles (general linear algebra; the paper bases it on the Grassmann distance). Each squared cosine is at most 1, so the sum is at most $\min(i,j)$, and dividing by it gives the average squared cosine, a number from 0 to 1. My checks: identical 2-direction sets give $2/2=1$; the sets $\{e_1,e_2\}$ and $\{e_1,e_3\}$ give $1/2=0.5$; two single directions at cosine 0.6 give $0.36$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What does LoRA add to a Transformer, and which matrices does it adapt in the paper?::It freezes chosen weight matrices and puts a small trainable pair of matrices beside each, whose output is added to the big matrix's output. In the experiments the pairs are on the attention query ($W_q$) and value ($W_v$) matrices of every layer; the other attention matrices, the feed-forward layers, LayerNorm and biases stay frozen "for simplicity and parameter-efficiency" (paper, 4.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm In LoRA, what do matrix $A$ and matrix $B$ each do, and how do they start?::$A$ ($r\times k$) shrinks the input's $k$ numbers to $r$ numbers and starts as random Gaussian numbers. $B$ ($d\times r$) grows those $r$ numbers back to $d$ and starts at zero, so $BA=0$ and the model begins exactly as the pre-trained one (paper, 4.1). The random $A$ makes sure $B$ gets a gradient at once (my reasoning).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What is the evidence that a LoRA rank as small as 1 can be enough?::Table 6 (GPT-3, $W_q$ and $W_v$ adapted): WikiSQL accuracy 73.4 at $r=1$, 73.3, 73.7, 73.8 at $r=8$ and 73.5 at $r=64$, flat within the noise of $\pm0.5$. Also the top singular direction learned at $r=8$ overlaps strongly with the one at $r=64$. With $W_q$ alone it needs more (68.8 at $r=1$, 70.5 at $r=4$). The authors do not expect small $r$ to work for every task.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Which attention matrices should LoRA adapt, according to the paper's Table 5?::With the same budget of about 18M trained values on GPT-3: $W_q$ alone 70.4 on WikiSQL, $W_k$ alone 70.0, $W_v$ alone 73.0, $W_o$ alone 73.2, $W_q$ with $W_v$ at rank 4 73.7, all four at rank 2 73.7 (and 91.7 on MultiNLI against 91.3). It is better to spread a small rank over more matrices than put a larger rank on one, but the paper's choice of $W_q$ with $W_v$ is not clearly the best.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What can you do with LoRA after training, and what is the limit?::Merge: compute $W=W_0+BA$ and run the model as usual, with no extra delay. Switch task: subtract $BA$ and add another $B'A'$, about 35 MB instead of 350 GB. The limit: after merging, inputs for different tasks cannot easily share one batch; not merging and picking the module per example is possible when latency is not critical (paper, 4.1 and 4.2).

At 2 bytes each that is about 37.7 million bytes (the paper says roughly 35 MB), against $175\times10^9\times2=350$ GB for the full model (my arithmetic). For 100 tasks: about 354 GB instead of about 35 TB (paper).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What are the steps of one LoRA layer in training?
?
1. The input $x$ enters both paths. 2. $A$ shrinks it to $r$ numbers: $Ax$. 3. $B$ grows it back: $BAx$. 4. Scale by $\alpha/r$ and add to the frozen output: $h=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAx$ (at the start $B=0$, so $h=W_0x$). 5. Backward: gradients are computed for $A$ and $B$ only; $W_0$ is not updated (the error still flows through $W_0$ to earlier layers).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm LoRA example at the first step: $x=(1,2,3)$, $A=(1,0,-1)$, $B=0$, $\alpha/r=2$, output error $g=(1,0.5)$. What are the gradients?
?
$Ax=-2$. The gradient for $B$ is $2\cdot g\cdot(-2)=(-4,-2)$, not zero. The gradient for $A$ is 0 because $B=0$. After one plain step with learning rate 0.1, $B=(0.4,0.2)$, and then $A$'s gradient is $2\cdot(0.4\cdot1+0.2\cdot0.5)\cdot x=(1,2,3)$. If $A$ had also started at zero, nothing would ever move (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What are the steps to serve a LoRA-adapted model and switch tasks?::Train $A$ and $B$ for a task; merge $W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$; run the model as usual (same shape and speed as the original); to switch task, subtract $\frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ and add $\frac{\alpha}{r}B'A'$. The example check: $W_0+2BA$ gives $Wx=(-3,-6)$, equal to the two-path result.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Is LoRA just fine-tuning fewer layers?::No. The paper's FTTop2 baseline trains only GPT-2 medium's last two layers: 25.19M trained values, 68.1 BLEU on E2E, against LoRA's 70.4 BLEU with 0.35M values (Table 3). LoRA touches every layer but only through a small low-rank change.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Does a low rank limit what LoRA can learn?::For one matrix yes, by design: $BA$ changes the weights in only $r$ directions. The paper says that applying LoRA to all matrices, training the biases and setting $r$ to the rank of the pre-trained matrices roughly recovers the expressiveness of full fine-tuning, and rank 1 already worked in GPT-3. The authors' caveat: a task in a different language from pre-training might need a larger $r$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What happens as you give LoRA more and more trained parameters, compared with adapters and prefix tuning?::According to the paper, LoRA roughly converges to training the original model, adapter methods converge to an MLP, and prefix-based methods to a model that cannot take long input sequences (paper, 4.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm How does LoRA differ from adapter layers?::Adapters are inserted in sequence with a nonlinearity between two small layers, so they add depth, cannot be folded into a matrix and cost +20.7% (AdapterL) and +30.3% (AdapterH) time at batch 1 and length 128 (Table 1). LoRA's pair is parallel and linear, so it merges and adds no latency.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm How does LoRA differ from prompt or prefix tuning?::Those train special input tokens or activations instead of weights. The paper says they use up part of the sequence length, and performance is not monotonic in trained parameters: it dropped with more than 256 special tokens (prefix-embedding) or more than 32 (prefix-layer) (paper, 5.5 and Figure 2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What does it mean that LoRA's $\Delta W$ "amplifies features"?::At $r=4$ in layer 48 of GPT-3, $\lVert W_q\rVert_F=61.95$ and $\lVert\Delta W_q\rVert_F=6.91$. $\Delta W$ correlates with $W$ more than random does but points at directions $W$ does not emphasise, amplifying them by about 21.5 ($6.91/0.32$). The paper's reading: LoRA boosts features learned in pre-training but not emphasised (paper, 7.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What is LoRA, and what is it not?::A way of fine-tuning a pre-trained model: the original weights are frozen and only a small extra pair of matrices $A$ and $B$ next to chosen matrices is trained. It is not pre-training and not a new kind of model; it only changes how a model is taught a new task.
