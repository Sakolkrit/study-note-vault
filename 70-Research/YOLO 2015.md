# YOLO 2015

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** Older detectors looked at hundreds of little pieces of a picture one by one. YOLO looks at the whole picture once, cuts it into a grid, and every grid square shouts out "I see this thing here, with this box, and this sure am I".

Paper: "You Only Look Once: Unified, Real-Time Object Detection", arXiv 1506.02640, the version read is v5 of 9 May 2016 (the first version came out in June 2015, from memory), 10 pages. Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my own arithmetic, done in Python. This paper's arXiv licence is not open (the usual arXiv licence), so its own figures are not copied. I searched Wikimedia Commons for a free YOLO figure and found none that fits, so the note has no picture; Figures 1 to 3 are described in words and with a size table.

## The problem
Detectors of the time were slow pipelines. They took a classifier and ran it "at various locations and scales" of the image (sliding window, as in deformable parts models), or, like [[R-CNN 2013]], first proposed about 2,000 boxes, classified each one, then cleaned up the boxes and removed duplicates. "These complex pipelines are slow and hard to optimize because each individual component must be trained separately" (paper, 1). The best of them, [[Fast R-CNN 2015]], ran at 0.5 frames per second in the paper's own test (paper, Table 1). Real-time detection, meaning 30 frames per second or better, existed only for a weak detector (30 Hz deformable parts model at 26.1 mAP) (paper, 4.1). See [[Object Detection]].

## What people did before
- **Deformable parts models (DPM):** a sliding window with hand-made features, a classifier, then a separate step for boxes. A disjoint pipeline (paper, 3).
- **[[R-CNN 2013]]:** Selective Search proposes boxes, a convolutional network gives features, an SVM scores them, a linear model fixes the boxes and [[Non-Maximum Suppression]] removes duplicates. More than 40 seconds per image at test time (paper, 3).
- **[[Fast R-CNN 2015]] and [[Faster R-CNN 2015]]:** share the computation and (for Faster) use a network to propose regions, but "both still fall short of real-time performance" (paper, 3).
- **Deep MultiBox and OverFeat:** MultiBox predicts regions of interest with a network but is a piece of a larger pipeline; OverFeat does sliding-window detection with a localizer that only sees local information (paper, 3).
- **MultiGrasp:** the grid idea comes from the authors' own earlier work on predicting where a robot can grasp an object, a much simpler task (paper, 3).

## The idea
Throw the pipeline away and treat detection as one regression problem, "straight from image pixels to bounding box coordinates and class probabilities" (paper, 1). One convolutional network looks at the full image once and outputs, for a 7 by 7 grid, a box guess, a confidence and a class guess in every square. The whole thing is trained end to end on a loss that is tied to detection. Because the network sees the whole image, it also "implicitly encodes contextual information" about the scene (paper, 1). The price is that it is worse than the best detectors at placing boxes precisely, especially small ones (paper, 1). See [[Convolutional Neural Network]] and [[Regression]].

## The architecture, part by part

The paper's Figure 1 (the three steps: resize to 448 by 448, run one network, threshold and suppress duplicates), Figure 2 (the grid, the boxes, the class map) and Figure 3 (the network) are not reproduced, since they are not free. Their content is given below in words and in a table.

The whole design (paper, 2 and 2.1). The image is resized to 448 by 448 and divided into an $S\times S$ grid with $S=7$. Each grid cell predicts $B=2$ boxes, each with 5 numbers ($x$, $y$, $w$, $h$ and a confidence), and one set of $C=20$ class probabilities (for the 20 classes of PASCAL VOC), so the answer is a $7\times7\times30$ tensor, or 1,470 numbers per image (the 30 is $2\times5+20$, my check). The network has 24 convolutional layers and 2 fully connected layers. A smaller "Fast YOLO" has 9 convolutional layers instead of 24 and fewer filters; all other settings are the same (paper, 2.1).

### Part by part
- **The grid and "responsibility".**
  - *What it does:* the image is cut into $7\times7$ cells, and "if the center of an object falls into a grid cell, that grid cell is responsible for detecting that object" (paper, 2).
  - *Why it matters:* it settles who predicts what. Each object has one responsible cell, which cuts down duplicate detections and replaces the 2,000 proposals of R-CNN by only 98 boxes per image (paper, 3).
- **The box predictions: $x$, $y$, $w$, $h$, confidence.**
  - *What they do:* $(x,y)$ is the centre of the box measured inside its grid cell, and $w$ and $h$ are measured relative to the whole image, so all four are between 0 and 1. The confidence is the predicted overlap ([[Intersection over Union]]) between the box and any true box (paper, 2 and 2.2).
  - *Why it matters:* normalising all four to 0 to 1 makes them easy for a regression layer to learn (paper, 2.2).
- **Two boxes per cell, one class set per cell.**
  - *What it does:* every cell proposes $B=2$ boxes but predicts only one set of $C$ class probabilities, "regardless of the number of boxes" (paper, 2).
  - *Why it matters:* it is a strong limit: a cell can only name one class, so two different objects whose centres fall in one cell cannot both be found (paper, 2.4).
- **The confidence and the class-specific score.**
  - *What it does:* the box confidence is meant to be the probability that an object is there times the overlap with the truth, and at test time it is multiplied with the cell's class probability (equation 1 below).
  - *Why it matters:* the final score says both "how sure am I that this class is here" and "how well does the box fit" in one number.
- **The "responsible predictor" within a cell.**
  - *What it does:* during training, of the two boxes in a cell, the one with the highest current overlap with the truth is made responsible for that object (paper, 2.2).
  - *Why it matters:* "this leads to specialization between the bounding box predictors": each predictor gets better at certain sizes, shapes or classes, which raises recall (paper, 2.2).
- **Pretraining on ImageNet at half resolution.**
  - *What it does:* the first 20 convolutional layers, followed by an average-pooling layer and a fully connected layer, were trained for about a week on the 1000-class ImageNet task with 224 by 224 images, reaching 88% single-crop top-5 accuracy on the validation set (paper, 2.2). Then 4 convolutional and 2 fully connected layers with random weights are added, and the input resolution is doubled to 448 by 448 "because detection often requires fine-grained visual information" (paper, 2.2). See [[Pre-training]] and [[Transfer Learning]].
  - *Why it matters:* the convolutions start from good general image features, and the detection part only has to learn where things are.
- **The output activation and the leaky ReLU.**
  - *What it does:* the last layer is linear (no squashing) and every other layer uses a leaky rectified linear unit (equation 2 below).
  - *Why it matters:* the leak keeps a small gradient for negative values, so units cannot die (general knowledge). See [[Activation Function]] and [[ReLU]].
- **Non-maximum suppression at the end.**
  - *What it does:* a big object, or one near the border of several cells, can be found by several cells; NMS removes the duplicates (paper, 2.3).
  - *Why it matters:* "while not critical to performance as it is for R-CNN or DPM, non-maximal suppression adds 2-3% in mAP" (paper, 2.3). See [[Non-Maximum Suppression]].
- **Fast YOLO.**
  - *What it does:* the same recipe with 9 convolutional layers instead of 24 and fewer filters (paper, 2.1).
  - *Why it matters:* it reaches 155 frames per second with 52.7 mAP, "more than twice as accurate as prior work on real-time detection" (paper, 4.1).

## The core mechanism, step by step

### From a picture to boxes (my numbered paraphrase of paper, 2 and 2.3)
1. **Resize** the image to 448 by 448.
2. **Run the network once.** It outputs the $7\times7\times30$ tensor.
3. **Read each cell:** two boxes with a confidence each, and 20 class probabilities.
4. **Multiply** each class probability by each box's confidence (equation 1) to get $98\times20=1{,}960$ class-specific scores (mine).
5. **Threshold** the low scores away and apply non-maximum suppression to remove duplicates.

**Worked example (tiny numbers, mine).** An object's centre is at pixel (300, 150) of the 448 by 448 image, and its box is 160 pixels wide and 220 pixels tall.
- A cell is $448/7=64$ pixels wide, so the centre lies in column $\lfloor300/64\rfloor=4$ and row $\lfloor150/64\rfloor=2$ (counting from 0).
- Inside that cell the centre is at $x=(300\bmod64)/64=0.688$ and $y=(150\bmod64)/64=0.344$ of the way across.
- The size relative to the whole image is $w=160/448=0.357$ and $h=220/448=0.491$.
- So the truth for the responsible cell is $(x,y,w,h)=(0.688,0.344,0.357,0.491)$, and every number lies between 0 and 1, as the paper says.

### Training (paper, 2.2)
1. **Pretrain** the first 20 convolutional layers on ImageNet at 224 by 224.
2. **Add** 4 convolutional and 2 fully connected layers with random weights and double the input to 448 by 448.
3. **Train for about 135 epochs** on the PASCAL VOC 2007 and 2012 training and validation sets (and, when testing on 2012, also the VOC 2007 test data), with batch size 64, momentum 0.9 and weight decay 0.0005 (paper, 2.2). See [[Momentum]] and [[Regularization]].
4. **Schedule the learning rate:** raise it slowly from 0.001 to 0.01 in the first epochs ("if we start at a high learning rate our model often diverges due to unstable gradients"), then 0.01 for 75 epochs, 0.001 for 30 and 0.0001 for 30 (paper, 2.2). See [[Learning Rate]]. The last three add up to 135 epochs (mine), so the warm-up seems to be counted inside the first 75, a small ambiguity in the text.
5. **Guard against overfitting** with dropout of 0.5 after the first fully connected layer, and data augmentation: random scaling and translation of up to 20% of the image size, and random exposure and saturation changes of up to a factor of 1.5 in HSV colour space (paper, 2.2). See [[Dropout]] and [[Data Augmentation]].
6. **Minimise the multi-part sum-squared loss** (equation 3).

## The equations

**1. The class-specific confidence at test time** (paper, Eq. 1)

$$
\Pr(\text{Class}_i\mid\text{Object})\cdot\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot\text{IOU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}=\Pr(\text{Class}_i)\cdot\text{IOU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}
$$

**Where:**
- $\Pr(\text{Class}_i\mid\text{Object})$: the cell's probability of class $i$, given that an object is in the cell; the network predicts it once per cell.
- $\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot\text{IOU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}$: the box's confidence. It is meant to be 0 when no object is in the cell, and otherwise equal to the overlap (intersection over union) between the predicted box and the true box.
- $\Pr(\text{Class}_i)\cdot\text{IOU}$: the final class-specific score for one box.

**Matters:** the product ranks boxes by both "is this class here" and "does the box fit". Without the overlap factor a loose box around the right object would score as high as a tight one; without the class factor a box would not say which class it is. See [[Intersection over Union]].

**Example (tiny numbers):** a cell says the object is a dog with probability 0.8, and the box has a predicted confidence of 0.7.
- Score $=0.8\times0.7=0.56$.
- A second box in the same cell with confidence 0.2 gets $0.8\times0.2=0.16$.
- Meaning: the first box is kept with score 0.56, and the sloppier one is much lower, so NMS and the threshold will drop it.

**2. The leaky ReLU activation** (paper, Eq. 2)

$$
\phi(x)=\begin{cases}x,&\text{if }x>0\\0.1\,x,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

**Where:**
- $x$: the input to the unit (the weighted sum from the layer below).
- $\phi(x)$: the output of the unit.
- $0.1$: the slope for negative inputs, the "leak".

**Matters:** an ordinary ReLU outputs 0 for every negative input and gives no gradient there, so a unit that is always negative never learns again. The leak keeps a small slope of 0.1. The paper uses this on all layers but the last (paper, 2.2); the reason for the choice is general knowledge, the paper gives none.

**Example (tiny numbers):** inputs $-4$, $0$ and $3$.
- $\phi(-4)=0.1\times(-4)=-0.4$ (a plain ReLU gives 0).
- $\phi(0)=0$ (the "otherwise" branch gives $0.1\times0$).
- $\phi(3)=3$.
- Meaning: positive values pass through unchanged and negative values are shrunk to a tenth, not erased.

**3. The multi-part loss** (paper, Eq. 3)

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\lambda_{\text{coord}}\sum_{i=0}^{S^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{B}\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\Big[(x_i-\hat x_i)^2+(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\Big]\\
+\;&\lambda_{\text{coord}}\sum_{i=0}^{S^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{B}\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\Big[\big(\sqrt{w_i}-\sqrt{\hat w_i}\big)^2+\big(\sqrt{h_i}-\sqrt{\hat h_i}\big)^2\Big]\\
+\;&\sum_{i=0}^{S^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{B}\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\big(C_i-\hat C_i\big)^2
+\lambda_{\text{noobj}}\sum_{i=0}^{S^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{B}\mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}\big(C_i-\hat C_i\big)^2\\
+\;&\sum_{i=0}^{S^{2}}\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i}\sum_{c\in\text{classes}}\big(p_i(c)-\hat p_i(c)\big)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

**Where:**
- $i$ runs over the $S^2=49$ grid cells and $j$ over the $B=2$ boxes of a cell (the paper's sum writes $B$ as the upper limit; the boxes are really counted from 1 to $B$, my reading).
- $x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i$: the true box centre, width and height; the hatted symbols $\hat x_i$ and so on are the network's prediction.
- $C_i$ and $\hat C_i$: the true and predicted confidence of the box; $p_i(c)$ and $\hat p_i(c)$: the true and predicted probability of class $c$ in cell $i$.
- $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}$: 1 if box $j$ of cell $i$ is the "responsible" predictor for an object, otherwise 0. $\mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}$ is 1 if it is not responsible (no object). $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i}$: 1 if an object appears in cell $i$.
- $\lambda_{\text{coord}}=5$ and $\lambda_{\text{noobj}}=0.5$: weights set by the paper (paper, 2.2).
- The five lines in order: box position, box size, confidence of boxes with an object, confidence of boxes without an object, class probabilities.

**Matters:** it is a plain sum-squared error, "easy to optimize", with three fixes, each explained by the paper.
- *The two weights.* Most cells hold no object, and their confidence would be pushed to 0, "often overpowering the gradient from cells that do contain objects", which can make training diverge early. So the loss on the position of boxes is raised ($\lambda_{\text{coord}}=5$) and the loss on the confidence of empty boxes is lowered ($\lambda_{\text{noobj}}=0.5$).
- *The square roots of $w$ and $h$.* Plain squared error would count an error in a big box the same as in a small one, but "small deviations in large boxes matter less than in small boxes". The square root partly fixes that.
- *The indicators.* The class term only counts if an object is in the cell, and the position terms only count for the responsible box, so a cell is not punished for classes or coordinates of an object that is not there.

**Example (tiny numbers):** one responsible box with true $(x,y,w,h)=(0.6,0.4,0.36,0.49)$ and predicted $(0.5,0.4,0.64,0.25)$. The true confidence is 0.7, the predicted one 0.5. One other box in the cell is empty and predicts confidence 0.3 where it should be 0. The class truth is (dog, cat, bird) = (1, 0, 0), predicted (0.8, 0.1, 0.1). Using $\lambda_{\text{coord}}=5$ and $\lambda_{\text{noobj}}=0.5$.
- *Position:* $5\times[(0.6-0.5)^2+(0.4-0.4)^2]=5\times0.01=0.05$.
- *Size:* $\sqrt{0.36}=0.6$, $\sqrt{0.64}=0.8$, $\sqrt{0.49}=0.7$, $\sqrt{0.25}=0.5$, so $5\times[(0.6-0.8)^2+(0.7-0.5)^2]=5\times(0.04+0.04)=0.40$.
- *Confidence, object box:* $(0.7-0.5)^2=0.04$.
- *Confidence, empty box:* $0.5\times(0-0.3)^2=0.5\times0.09=0.045$.
- *Class:* $(1-0.8)^2+(0-0.1)^2+(0-0.1)^2=0.04+0.01+0.01=0.06$.
- *Total:* $0.05+0.40+0.04+0.045+0.06=0.595$.
- Meaning: the biggest part of this loss is the size error, 0.40 of 0.595, because the predicted box was too wide and too short; the empty box costs only 0.045 because it is down-weighted by half.

## What they found
The headline numbers (paper): the base YOLO runs at 45 frames per second with 63.4 mAP on PASCAL VOC 2007; Fast YOLO runs at 155 frames per second with 52.7 mAP; YOLO makes more localisation errors than Fast R-CNN but fewer false positives on background, and it generalises better to artwork (abstract; paper, 4).

### Using YOLO to fix Fast R-CNN (paper's Table 2)

**Table 2: model combination on VOC 2007** (written by me from the paper's Table 2; all rows shown).

| Model | mAP alone | mAP combined with the best Fast R-CNN | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fast R-CNN (best, 71.8) | 71.8 | not applicable | not applicable |
| Fast R-CNN (2007 data) | 66.9 | 72.4 | 0.6 |
| Fast R-CNN (VGG-M) | 59.2 | 72.4 | 0.6 |
| Fast R-CNN (CaffeNet) | 57.1 | 72.1 | 0.3 |
| YOLO | 63.4 | 75.0 | 3.2 |

What each column is, and why it matters:
- **mAP alone:** that model's own score. **Combined:** for every box the Fast R-CNN predicts, check whether YOLO predicts a similar box, and if so boost the score by YOLO's probability and the overlap (paper, 4.3). **Gain:** combined minus the 71.8 of the best Fast R-CNN alone.

**Summary: what the table shows.**
- **Best:** YOLO gives +3.2 (75.0), about 4.5% in relative terms (mine). Other Fast R-CNN versions add only 0.3 to 0.6.
- **The paper's argument:** this is "not simply a byproduct of model ensembling"; YOLO helps because it "makes different kinds of mistakes".
- **Caution:** it does not keep YOLO's speed, since each model runs separately, though YOLO adds little time (paper, 4.3).

## Questions I had, answered

**Why does YOLO predict the square root of the width and height?** Squared error treats a 0.06 error in width the same for a tiny box and a huge one, but 0.06 matters far more for a tiny box. My numbers: a width of 0.9 predicted as 0.84 and a width of 0.1 predicted as 0.04 both have a raw squared error of $0.0036$; with square roots it becomes 0.00103 for the big box and 0.01351 for the small one, 13 times as much (mine). The paper says this "partially" addresses the problem (paper, 2.2).

**What happens to cells with no object, and why the 0.5?** There are 49 cells and most have nothing in them. If all their confidences counted fully, those 97 empty boxes (of 98, when one object is present) would drown out the one box that counts. In my tiny example, with a confidence of 0.3 on each empty box, they cost $97\times0.09=8.73$ at weight 1 and half that, 4.37, at weight 0.5 (mine). The paper only says it lowers the weight so training does not diverge; it gives no ablation of the value 0.5 or of the 5.

**Why does a grid cell only get one class?** It keeps the output small and the design simple, and the paper says so as a limitation: "each grid cell only predicts two boxes and can only have one class", so it "struggles with small objects that appear in groups, such as flocks of birds" (paper, 2.4).

**Is the 135 epochs a lot?** About 135 passes over the roughly 16.5 thousand VOC 2007+2012 trainval images (the image count is from memory, not the paper) after a week of pretraining on ImageNet (paper, 2.2). I did not recompute the training time.

**If the network sees the whole image, why is localisation its weak point?** The paper's reasons: strong spatial constraints (2 boxes per cell), coarse features because of the many downsampling layers, difficulty with new aspect ratios, and a loss that treats errors in small and large boxes alike (paper, 2.4). It names incorrect localisations as "our main source of error".

## Why it mattered
It showed that a single network, trained end to end, can do detection fast enough for video, 45 frames per second against 0.5 for Fast R-CNN, and started the "one-stage" line of detectors (a general-knowledge statement, not made in the paper) as a rival to the two-stage line of [[R-CNN 2013]] to [[Faster R-CNN 2015]] and [[Mask R-CNN 2017]]. It also showed that a fast detector can make very different mistakes from a slow one, so the two can help each other (the Fast R-CNN + YOLO result).

## Limits and open questions
- **Localisation:** the main source of error (paper, 2.4 and 4.2); 19.0% of YOLO's top detections are mislocated, against 8.6% for Fast R-CNN.
- **Small objects and crowds:** one class and two boxes per cell, so groups of small objects are missed (paper, 2.4); on VOC 2012 it lags on bottle, sheep and tv (paper, 4.4).
- **Accuracy gap:** 63.4 mAP against 73.2 for Faster R-CNN VGG-16 on VOC 2007, and 57.9 against 68.4 for Fast R-CNN on VOC 2012 (paper; the gaps are mine).
- **New shapes:** it "struggles to generalize to objects in new or unusual aspect ratios" (paper, 2.4).
- **Not shown in the paper:** the effect of $\lambda_{\text{coord}}$, $\lambda_{\text{noobj}}$ or of the grid size, since no ablation is given; the paper trains only on VOC, and tests only on person for the art sets.
- **The loss isn't the metric:** the sum-squared error "does not perfectly align with our goal of maximizing average precision" (paper, 2.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation YOLO (Redmon, Divvala, Girshick and Farhadi, 2015, arXiv 1506.02640): what problem did it attack?::Detection pipelines were slow and hard to optimise (sliding windows, or R-CNN's 2,000 proposals plus a classifier plus clean-up), with each part trained separately. Fast R-CNN ran at 0.5 fps and only a weak DPM was real time (paper, 1 and Table 1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What did people do before YOLO?::Sliding-window DPM, R-CNN (Selective Search plus a CNN plus SVMs, more than 40 seconds per image), and Fast and Faster R-CNN, which are faster but not real time (paper, 3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation YOLO: what is the idea?::Treat detection as one regression problem from pixels to boxes and class probabilities: one network looks at the full image once, on a 7 by 7 grid, and the whole system is trained end to end on a detection loss (paper, 1 and 2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What does YOLO's output tensor contain, and how big is it?::A $7\times7\times30$ tensor: per grid cell 2 boxes with 5 numbers each ($x$, $y$, $w$, $h$, confidence) and 20 class probabilities, since $2\times5+20=30$; 1,470 numbers and 98 boxes per image (paper, 2; arithmetic mine).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What is a grid cell responsible for in YOLO?::An object belongs to the cell that contains its centre, and that cell must detect it (paper, 2). This avoids many duplicates and gives only 98 boxes instead of R-CNN's 2,000.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How are YOLO's box numbers parametrised, and why?::$(x,y)$ is the box centre as an offset inside its cell, and $w,h$ are relative to the whole image, so all four lie between 0 and 1, which makes them easy to regress (paper, 2 and 2.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Example: an object's centre is at pixel (300, 150) in the 448 by 448 image with a 160 by 220 box. Which cell and what are the four numbers?
?
A cell is $448/7=64$ pixels, so column 4 and row 2 (counting from 0). Inside the cell $x=0.688$ and $y=0.344$; relative to the image $w=160/448=0.357$ and $h=220/448=0.491$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What does YOLO's network look like?::24 convolutional layers followed by 2 fully connected ones, inspired by GoogLeNet but with 1 by 1 reduction layers followed by 3 by 3 convolutions instead of inception modules. The map shrinks 448, 112, 56, 28, 14, 7 (paper, 2.1; sizes mine).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does YOLO use alternating 1 by 1 and 3 by 3 convolutions?::The 1 by 1 layers reduce the number of channels so the 3 by 3 convolution after them is cheaper; this replaces GoogLeNet's inception modules (paper, 2.1; the reason for the saving is general knowledge).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How was YOLO pretrained and why was the resolution doubled?::The first 20 convolutional layers were trained for about a week on ImageNet at 224 by 224 (88% single-crop top-5), then 4 convolutional and 2 fully connected layers were added and the input went to 448 by 448 because detection needs fine-grained visual information (paper, 2.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How big is YOLO, by my count?::About 60.2 M weights in the convolutions and 211.5 M in the two fully connected layers, 271.7 M in all, 78% in the two fully connected layers (75.6% in the first); about 20 billion multiply-adds per image. My reconstruction from the paper's Figure 3 labels; the paper gives no count in the text I read.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What is Fast YOLO?::The same recipe with 9 convolutional layers instead of 24 and fewer filters; it runs at 155 fps with 52.7 mAP, more than twice the mAP of other real-time detectors (paper, 2.1 and 4.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Which cell box becomes the "responsible predictor" during training, and why?::The box with the highest current overlap (IOU) with the truth. It makes the box predictors specialise in sizes, aspect ratios or classes, which improves recall (paper, 2.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does YOLO still need non-maximum suppression?::A large object, or one near a cell border, can be found by several cells. NMS removes the duplicates; it is not critical, as it is for R-CNN or DPM, but adds 2 to 3 mAP (paper, 2.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write YOLO's class-specific confidence (paper's Eq. 1) and explain each part.
?
$$\Pr(\text{Class}_i\mid\text{Object})\cdot\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot\text{IOU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}=\Pr(\text{Class}_i)\cdot\text{IOU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}$$
- $\Pr(\text{Class}_i\mid\text{Object})$: the cell's class probability given an object; $\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot\text{IOU}$: the box confidence (0 if no object, otherwise the overlap with the truth).
- The product ranks boxes by both "is this class here" and "does the box fit".

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Class score example: class probability 0.8 and box confidence 0.7, then another box with confidence 0.2. What are the scores?
?
$0.8\times0.7=0.56$ and $0.8\times0.2=0.16$ (my arithmetic). The sloppy box scores much lower and is dropped by the threshold or NMS.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why multiply the class probability by the box confidence?
?
The class probability alone says what is in the cell but not how good the box is; the confidence alone says the box fits but not which class. The product carries both: with class 0.8 a box with confidence 0.7 scores 0.56 and one with 0.2 scores 0.16, so tight boxes win (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write YOLO's leaky ReLU (paper's Eq. 2) and explain each symbol.
?
$$\phi(x)=\begin{cases}x,&\text{if }x>0\\0.1\,x,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
- $x$: the input to the unit; $\phi(x)$: its output; $0.1$: the slope for negative inputs.
- It lets a small negative signal through, so units cannot die, which a plain ReLU does (the reason is general knowledge).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Leaky ReLU example: inputs $-4$, $0$ and $3$. What are the outputs?
?
$-0.4$, $0$ and $3$ (my arithmetic). Positive values pass unchanged and negative values are shrunk to a tenth, not erased.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does the leaky ReLU use 0.1 and a straight line, and what breaks with a plain ReLU?
?
A negative input of $-4$ gives $-0.4$ with the leak and exactly $0$ with a plain ReLU, with no gradient, so a unit stuck below zero stops learning. The paper uses it everywhere except the last layer, which is linear, and gives no reason or ablation, so the explanation is general knowledge.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write YOLO's loss in words, part by part (paper's Eq. 3).
?
$$\lambda_{\text{coord}}\sum\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\big[(x-\hat x)^2+(y-\hat y)^2\big]+\lambda_{\text{coord}}\sum\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\big[(\sqrt{w}-\sqrt{\hat w})^2+(\sqrt{h}-\sqrt{\hat h})^2\big]+\sum\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}(C-\hat C)^2+\lambda_{\text{noobj}}\sum\mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}(C-\hat C)^2+\sum\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i}\sum_{c}(p(c)-\hat p(c))^2$$
- Five sums over the 49 cells and their boxes: position, size (square roots), confidence of object boxes, confidence of empty boxes, class probabilities. Hats are predictions.
- $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}$ is 1 for the responsible box, $\mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}$ for the others, $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i}$ for a cell with an object; $\lambda_{\text{coord}}=5$ and $\lambda_{\text{noobj}}=0.5$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Loss example: true box $(0.6,0.4,0.36,0.49)$, predicted $(0.5,0.4,0.64,0.25)$, object confidence 0.7 against 0.5, one empty box at 0.3, classes truth (1,0,0) against (0.8,0.1,0.1). What is the loss?
?
Position $5\times0.01=0.05$; size $5\times(0.04+0.04)=0.40$; object confidence $0.04$; empty box $0.5\times0.09=0.045$; class $0.04+0.01+0.01=0.06$. Total $0.595$, mostly the size error (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does the YOLO loss have $\lambda_{\text{coord}}=5$ and $\lambda_{\text{noobj}}=0.5$?
?
Most cells have no object and would push their confidence to 0 and overpower the cells that do, which can make training diverge early. So the position loss is raised (5) and the empty-box confidence loss lowered (0.5). With 97 empty boxes at confidence 0.3 the empty-box cost is 8.73 at weight 1 and 4.37 at 0.5 (my arithmetic). The paper gives no ablation of the values.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does the YOLO loss use the square root of the width and height?
?
Squared error counts a width error the same for small and large boxes, but a small error in a small box matters far more. A width of 0.9 predicted as 0.84 and a width of 0.1 predicted as 0.04 both cost $0.0036$ raw, but with square roots $0.00103$ against $0.01351$, 13 times as much for the small box (my arithmetic). The paper says this only partly fixes it.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does the YOLO loss have the indicator terms?
?
So a cell is only punished for the class of an object that is present, and only the responsible box is punished for its coordinates. Without them the empty cells would pull every prediction toward zero, and the unused box in a cell would be pushed toward the truth box.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How was YOLO trained?::About 135 epochs on VOC 2007 and 2012 (batch 64, momentum 0.9, decay 0.0005); learning rate raised slowly from 0.001 to 0.01, then 0.01 for 75 epochs, 0.001 for 30 and 0.0001 for 30; dropout 0.5 after the first fully connected layer; augmentation by scaling and translation up to 20% and HSV changes up to a factor of 1.5 (paper, 2.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How did YOLO compare in speed and accuracy on VOC 2007 (paper's Table 1: best, worst, caution)?::Fast YOLO 52.7 mAP at 155 fps; YOLO 63.4 at 45 fps; Faster R-CNN VGG-16 best at 73.2 but 7 fps; Fast R-CNN 70.0 at 0.5 fps; 100 Hz DPM worst at 16.0. YOLO is 9.8 points below and 6.4 times faster than Faster R-CNN VGG-16 (mine). Caution: the YOLO authors measured the speeds; the Faster R-CNN paper reports 5 fps against 7 here.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What are the different kinds of mistakes of YOLO and Fast R-CNN (paper's Figure 4)?::YOLO has 19.0% localisation errors against 8.6% for Fast R-CNN (2.2 times as many), but only 4.75% background errors against 13.6% (Fast R-CNN has 2.9 times as many, mine).
