# GPT-3 2020

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** A very big next-word predictor that can do new tasks just from a few examples in the prompt, without being retrained.

Paper: "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners", arXiv 2005.14165, read as version 4 of 22 July 2020. Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my arithmetic. The paper's arXiv licence is not open and I found no free figures for it, so there are no pictures in this note; the tables are written by me. The paper is 75 pages; I read the main text and parts of the appendix (see the end), not the long appendix of per-task results.

## The problem
Pre-training followed by fine-tuning still needs "task-specific fine-tuning datasets of thousands or tens of thousands of examples" for each new task, while people can do a new language task from a few examples or a simple instruction (abstract). The paper gives three reasons this matters: a large labelled data set is hard to collect for every task; a model fine-tuned on a narrow data set can exploit spurious patterns and generalise poorly outside it; and people do not need such data (paper, 1).

## What people did before
Pre-train, then fine-tune on each task ([[BERT 2018]]). Earlier "in-context" attempts, such as GPT-2 used with its prompt only, got results "far inferior to fine-tuning": for example GPT-2 reached only 4% on Natural Questions (paper, 1). Language models had been growing: 100 million, 300 million, 1.5 billion, 8 billion, 11 billion and 17 billion parameters (paper, 1).

## The idea
Scale an autoregressive language model to 175 billion parameters, 10 times more than any previous non-sparse language model, and give tasks and examples purely as text in the prompt, with **no weight updates** ([[In-Context Learning]]). The paper's bet: abilities to learn a task from the context "might show similarly strong gains with scale" as the loss does (paper, 1).

## The architecture (figures)
There is no new architecture. GPT-3 is the same kind of model as GPT-2, only much bigger, trained on more data. This section explains what it is and which choices the paper made. Marks as above.

### Part by part
- **The model: a decoder-only Transformer, as in GPT-2.**
  - *What it does:* "the same model and architecture as GPT-2", including its modified initialisation, pre-normalisation and reversible tokenisation, "with the exception that we use alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns in the layers", like the Sparse Transformer (paper, 2.1). It predicts the next token given the earlier ones, so each token can only look at what came before ([[Transformer]], [[Self-Attention]]).
  - *Why it matters:* the left-to-right design lets the model be used by simply continuing a text, which is how tasks are given to it. The cost is that it cannot look ahead, which the paper links to its weak spots on tasks that compare two texts (limits below).
- **Eight sizes, from 125 million to 175 billion parameters.**
  - *What it does:* the paper trains 8 models over three orders of magnitude (Table 2.1 below); the largest is "GPT-3". All use a context window of 2,048 tokens and a feed-forward size of 4 times the width.
  - *Why it matters:* it lets the authors test the scaling hypothesis: whether loss and few-shot ability grow smoothly with size (paper, 2.1).
- **The training data mixture.**
  - *What it does:* a mixture of filtered Common Crawl (570 GB after filtering, about 400 billion tokens), an expanded WebText, two books corpora and Wikipedia (Table 2.2 below). The paper (1) filtered Common Crawl by similarity to high-quality reference texts, (2) removed near-duplicate documents within and across data sets, and (3) added curated corpora (paper, 2.2).
  - *Why it matters:* unfiltered web text is lower quality than curated text; deduplication also keeps the held-out validation set an honest measure of over-fitting. Higher-quality sources are sampled more often than their size: Common Crawl and Books2 are seen less than once, others two to three times, which "accepts a small amount of overfitting in exchange for higher quality training data" (paper, 2.2).
- **300 billion training tokens for every model.**
  - *What it does:* all 8 models see the same 300 billion tokens (Table 2.1).
  - *Why it matters:* following Kaplan et al. (2020), the paper trains "much larger models on many fewer tokens than is typical": GPT-3 "3B" took about the same compute as RoBERTa-Large though almost 10 times bigger (paper, Figure 2.2). Later work found this under-trained the models ([[Chinchilla 2022]]; that is Chinchilla's claim, not this paper's).
- **Batch size and learning rate that depend on size.**
  - *What it does:* larger models use a larger batch (0.5 million to 3.2 million tokens) and a smaller learning rate ($6.0\times10^{-4}$ down to $0.6\times10^{-4}$) (Table 2.1). Training used Adam ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$), gradient clipping at 1.0, a cosine decay of the learning rate to 10% over 260 billion tokens, a linear warm-up over the first 375 million tokens, and weight decay 0.1 (paper, appendix B).
  - *Why it matters:* the paper says larger models can use a larger batch but need a smaller learning rate, and it measured the gradient noise scale to pick the batch size (paper, 2.3).
- **Training hardware.**
  - *What it does:* V100 GPUs on a cluster provided by Microsoft, with the model split across GPUs both along depth and along width (paper, 2.1 and 2.3).
  - *Why it matters:* a 175-billion-parameter model does not fit on one device, so the split is needed.
- **No fine-tuning at test time.**
  - *What it does:* every task is given by text only, in the zero-, one- or few-shot settings (core mechanism below). The paper does not fine-tune GPT-3, "but GPT-3 can be fine-tuned in principle" (paper, 2).
  - *Why it matters:* that is the point of the paper: one model, no per-task training.

## The core mechanism, step by step

### In-context learning: a new task with no training
**The idea (my analogy).** Instead of teaching the model a task by changing its weights, show it the task the way you would show a person: say what you want, show a few solved examples, then give the new question and let it continue the pattern.

The paper compares four ways to use a model on a task (paper, 2 and Figure 2.1):

| Setting | Weights updated? | Examples used | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuning | yes | thousands to hundreds of thousands | strong, but needs a data set per task; not used for GPT-3 |
| Few-shot | no | $K$ in the prompt, usually 10 to 100 (what fits in 2,048 tokens) | worse than fine-tuned state of the art, so far |
| One-shot | no | exactly 1, plus an instruction | closest to how a task is explained to a person |
| Zero-shot | no | none, only a natural-language instruction | most convenient and hardest, sometimes "unfairly hard" |

*A table written by me from the paper's text.*

1. **Write the instruction (optional).**
   - *What it does:* a short natural-language description of the task, for example "Translate English to French:" (the example is mine; the paper's exact phrasings are in its appendix G).
   - *Why it matters:* for zero-shot it is the only task information; for few-shot the paper finds a task description also helps (Figure 1.2).
2. **Add $K$ solved examples.**
   - *What it does:* $K$ pairs of context and correct completion are drawn at random from the task's training set and placed in the prompt, separated by one or two newlines. $K$ ranges from 0 to what fits in the 2,048-token window, typically 10 to 100 (paper, 2.4). When separate development and test sets exist, a few values of $K$ are tried on the development set and the best is used.
   - *Why it matters:* the examples tell the model the task and the format of the answer, with no weight update. "Larger values of $K$ are usually but not always better" (paper, 2.4).
3. **Add the new input and let the model continue.**
   - *What it does:* the last example gives only the context; the model must produce the completion.
   - *Why it matters:* every task is turned into "predict what comes next", the model's one skill.
4. **Read off the answer.**
   - *What it does:* for multiple choice, compare the model's likelihood of each option (per-token, to normalise for length; Eq. 1 below for three data sets); for free-form answers, use beam search with a beam width of 4 and a length penalty of 0.6, scored by F1, BLEU or exact match (paper, 2.4).
   - *Why it matters:* it gives a definite answer without any task-specific output layer.

**Worked example (mine; a tiny few-shot prompt).**
```text
Translate English to French:
sea otter => loutre de mer
cheese => fromage
house =>
```
The model is expected to continue with " maison". No gradient is computed: only a forward pass over this prompt. The first line is the instruction, the second and third lines are $K=2$ examples, and the last line is the question.

**Why in-context ability matters for size (paper, 1 and Figure 1.2).** The paper finds that larger models make "increasingly efficient use of in-context information": on a task of removing random symbols from a word, the curve of accuracy against the number of examples is steeper for bigger models, with or without a task description.

## The equations
The paper has almost no displayed mathematics. Two formulas it states in the text are set out here so each term can be explained.

**1. The score for multiple-choice answers** (section 2.4)

$$
\text{score}(\text{completion})=\frac{P(\text{completion}\mid\text{context})}{P(\text{completion}\mid\text{answer context})}
$$

**Where:**
- $P(\text{completion}\mid\text{context})$: the probability the model gives to this answer text after the actual question and examples.
- $P(\text{completion}\mid\text{answer context})$: the probability it gives to the same answer after only a generic cue, the string "Answer:" or "A:".
- The paper uses this normalisation for ARC, OpenBookQA and RACE; for most tasks it compares the per-token likelihood instead.

**Matters:** an answer made of rare words has a low probability whatever the question. Dividing by its probability without the question removes that bias, so the score reflects how much the question raises the answer's probability. Without it, common-sounding wrong answers can win. The paper says it gave "additional benefit as measured on the development set" for those three data sets.

**Example (tiny numbers):** two answers. A: $P=0.02$ with the question, $0.01$ with only "Answer:". B: $0.01$ with the question, $0.001$ with only "Answer:".
- Raw likelihood prefers A (0.02 against 0.01).
- Normalised scores: A $=0.02/0.01=2$; B $=0.01/0.001=10$. B wins.
- Meaning: B is a rarer answer but the question makes it 10 times more likely, while it makes A only twice as likely.

**2. The training compute** (appendix D)

$$
C\approx6\,N\,D
$$

**Where:**
- $C$: total training FLOPs; $N$: number of parameters; $D$: number of training tokens (300 billion for every model).
- The 6 comes from 2 (a multiply and an add per parameter per token in the forward pass) times 3 (the backward pass is counted as twice the forward pass, so 3 in total); the paper ignores the attention operation, which it says uses under 10% of the compute (appendix D).

**Matters:** it turns size and data into a budget. In the paper's Figure 2.2 it shows that GPT-3 "3B" took about the same compute as RoBERTa-Large though it has about 10 times the parameters, because it saw far fewer tokens per parameter.

**Example (tiny numbers):** GPT-3 175B: $6\times174.6\times10^{9}\times300\times10^{9}=3.14\times10^{23}$ FLOPs.
- In petaflop/s-days (one is $8.64\times10^{19}$ FLOPs): $3.14\times10^{23}/8.64\times10^{19}=3{,}637$, the paper's $3.64\times10^{3}$.
- Meaning: the largest model took about 3,640 days at a petaflop per second, nearly 14 times the 13B model's 268 (my arithmetic).

## What they found
GPT-3 achieves "strong performance on many NLP datasets, including translation, question-answering, and cloze tasks, as well as several tasks that require on-the-fly reasoning or domain adaptation" in the few-shot setting, "sometimes even reaching competitiveness with prior state-of-the-art fine-tuning approaches" (abstract). It also finds weaknesses: natural language inference (ANLI), some reading comprehension (RACE, QuAC) and word-comparison tasks (WiC) (paper, 1 and 3). Language-modelling loss falls as a smooth power law in compute over a range extended by two more orders of magnitude, with only slight departure from the trend (paper, 3 and Figure 3.1, which I read as text only). The few-shot results are on 42 accuracy benchmarks, aggregated in the paper's Figure 1.3: zero-shot improves steadily with size and few-shot "increases more rapidly".

## Questions I had, answered

**Is few-shot prompting really "learning"?** The paper leaves it open. It says there is "ambiguity about whether few-shot learning actually learns new tasks from scratch at inference time, or if it simply recognizes and identifies tasks that it has learned during training", on a spectrum from demonstrations drawn from the training distribution to genuinely new tasks (paper, 5). The name "in-context learning" is intentionally neutral on this.

**Does the model change when I give it examples?** No. The weights stay fixed. The examples are only part of the input text; the model processes them in the same forward pass as the question (paper, 1 and 2).

**Why is GPT-3 weak at tasks like WiC and ANLI?** The paper's conjecture: it is a left-to-right model, which may cost it on tasks that compare two pieces of text or need re-reading, and that a large bidirectional model might be better at them (paper, 5). Compare [[BERT 2018]], which reads both sides. This is a conjecture; the paper did not test a bidirectional model of this scale.

**Was the benchmark contamination a problem?** The paper measured it carefully. It built cleaned versions of each benchmark and compared performance on the clean subset: for most benchmarks the change was negligible. Two were flagged and marked with an asterisk: PIQA (29% of examples flagged, a 3-point drop on the clean subset, probably a statistical bias because a 25 times smaller model shows a similar drop) and Winograd (45% flagged, a 2.6% drop; 132 Winograd schemas were found in the training set). The four Wikipedia language-modelling benchmarks and the Children's Book Test were almost entirely in the training data, so they were not reported. For reading-comprehension sets, source passages were in the training data but not the question-answer pairs. A limit: the clean subset might be easier or harder than the full set (paper, 4).

**Did it over-fit its training set?** The gap between training and validation loss is small and grows only minimally with model size and training time, so the paper argues that most of the gap comes from a difference in difficulty (paper, 4 and Figure 4.1).

**Why train for only 300 billion tokens?** Following Kaplan et al. (2020), the paper trained much larger models on fewer tokens than usual. Later work (the Chinchilla paper) argued that this left models under-trained for their size; that is a later paper's claim, not this paper's.

## Why it mattered
Showed that scale unlocks few-shot learning. Prompting became a way to use models ([[Prompt Engineering]]).

## Limits and open questions
The paper's own list (Section 5):
- **Text quality:** samples sometimes repeat themselves, lose coherence over long passages, contradict themselves or contain non-sequiturs.
- **Common-sense physics:** difficulty with questions such as "If I put cheese into the fridge, will it melt?".
- **Gaps in in-context learning:** close to chance on "comparison" tasks such as WiC and ANLI, and on some reading comprehension sets (RACE, QuAC).
- **Left-to-right design:** no bidirectional model was tried; the authors suggest one at this scale could do better on fill-in-the-blank, comparison and re-reading tasks.
- **The pre-training objective weights every token equally** and has no notion of what is most important to predict; tasks are forced into prediction; the model has no grounding in other kinds of experience such as images.
- **Poor sample efficiency in pre-training:** it sees far more text than a person ever does.
- **Few-shot ambiguity:** unclear whether it learns tasks or recognises them.
- **Interpretability, cost, bias and misuse:** discussed in the paper's later sections, which I read only in part (Section 6 not at all).
- **Contamination:** a bug left some test-set overlap in the training data (Section 4).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What are the steps of in-context learning in GPT-3?
?
1. Write an instruction (optional). 2. Add $K$ solved examples drawn from the task's training set, separated by newlines ($K$ up to what fits in 2,048 tokens). 3. Add the new input and let the model continue. 4. Read the answer: compare the likelihood of each option for multiple choice, or use beam search (width 4, length penalty 0.6) for free-form answers. #flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the multiple-choice score GPT-3 uses for ARC, OpenBookQA and RACE, and explain each symbol.
?
$$\text{score}(\text{completion})=\frac{P(\text{completion}\mid\text{context})}{P(\text{completion}\mid\text{answer context})}$$
- The numerator: the probability of the answer after the real question and examples. The denominator: its probability after only a generic cue ("Answer:" or "A:").
- Dividing removes the bias towards answers made of common words, so the score shows how much the question raises the answer's probability.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Score example: A has probability 0.02 with the question and 0.01 with only "Answer:"; B has 0.01 and 0.001. Which wins with the normalised score?
?
A: $0.02/0.01=2$. B: $0.01/0.001=10$. B wins, though the raw likelihood prefers A (0.02 against 0.01): the question makes B 10 times more likely and A only twice as likely (my arithmetic).
