# DPO 2023

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** The old way needed two stages and a lot of tuning to teach a model what people prefer. This paper found a trick that does it with one simple learning step on pairs of better and worse answers.

Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model", arXiv 2305.18290, read as version 3 of 29 July 2024 (NeurIPS 2023). Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my arithmetic. This paper's arXiv licence is CC BY 4.0, so its own figures are used below, cropped from the PDF, with credit to the authors (Rafailov and others).

## The problem
Language models trained on huge text learn a lot, but it is hard to steer their behaviour precisely, because "the completely unsupervised nature of their training" gives no say about which of the things they learned they should do (abstract). The standard cure, reinforcement learning from human feedback ([[RLHF]]), is "a complex and often unstable procedure": first fit a reward model of human preferences, then fine-tune the language model with reinforcement learning to maximise that reward "without drifting too far from the original model" (abstract). It involves training several language models and sampling from the model during training, at "significant computational cost" (paper, 1).

## What people did before
[[RLHF]] with a separate [[Reward Model]] and [[PPO]], as in [[InstructGPT 2022]]. Three phases: supervised fine-tuning, preference sampling and reward learning, and RL optimisation (paper, 3). Instruction tuning on human-written answers came before, but relative human judgments ("A is better than B") are "often easier to collect than expert demonstrations" (paper, 2).

## The idea
Skip the separate reward model and the reinforcement learning. The paper shows that the language model itself can play the role of the reward model: a change of variables turns the preference-learning loss into a loss on the language model's own probabilities. Then the whole thing is trained like a classifier on pairs of preferred and rejected answers ([[Direct Preference Optimization]], [[Bradley-Terry Model]]). "Your language model is secretly a reward model" (title).

## The method (the pipeline)
There is no new network. DPO is a training recipe for an existing language model, so this section explains the pieces and how they differ from RLHF. Marks as above.

### Part by part
- **The preference data.**
  - *What it does:* a data set of triples: a prompt $x$, a preferred answer $y_w$ ("winner") and a rejected answer $y_l$ ("loser"). In practice two answers are sampled from the model for each prompt and a human says which is better (paper, 3).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the only human signal used, and comparing two answers is easier for people than writing a perfect one (paper, 2). DPO works on a fixed, offline data set, "eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning" (abstract).
- **The reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.**
  - *What it does:* a frozen copy of the starting model, normally the supervised fine-tuned model ($\pi_{\text{SFT}}$). When no such model exists, it is made by maximising the likelihood of the preferred answers (paper, 4).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the anchor that stops the new model drifting; the loss measures how much more the new model likes each answer than the reference does.
- **The policy $\pi_\theta$.**
  - *What it does:* the language model being trained, started as a copy of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (paper, 3).
  - *Why it matters:* it is both the language model and, implicitly, the reward model; there is nothing else to train.
- **The temperature-like constant $\beta$.**
  - *What it does:* controls how far the policy is allowed to move from the reference. The paper uses $\beta=0.1$ by default (appendix B) and tried $0.05$, $0.1$, $1$ and $5$ in the sentiment sweep (paper, 6.1).
  - *Why it matters:* it sets the strength of the "stay close" constraint. Small $\beta$ allows big moves, large $\beta$ keeps the model near the reference (see the example under the equations).
- **The loss.**
  - *What it does:* a binary cross-entropy: it asks the policy to raise the probability of the preferred answer relative to the reference by more than it raises the rejected one (Eq. 7 below).
  - *Why it matters:* it is "a simple classification loss", so training is as easy as supervised learning, with no sampling loop and no separate reward model.
- **What it leaves out.**
  - *What it does:* no reward network and no reinforcement-learning loop (paper, abstract and 4).
  - *Why it matters:* these are the parts that make RLHF complex and unstable. The abstract says DPO eliminates the need for "significant hyperparameter tuning".

### Training recipe used in the paper (appendix B)
Unless noted otherwise: $\beta=0.1$, batch size 64, the RMSprop optimizer with learning rate $10^{-6}$, warmed up linearly from 0 over 150 steps (paper, B). Models used: GPT-2-large (sentiment), GPT-J 6B (summarization) and Pythia 2.8B (dialogue) (paper, 6). So the paper's evidence is for models of up to 6 billion parameters.

## The core mechanism, step by step

### From the RLHF objective to the DPO loss
**The idea (my paraphrase).** RLHF has two problems chained together: learn a reward, then maximise it. The paper notices that the best policy for a given reward has a closed form, so the reward can be written in terms of the policy. Put that into the preference model and the reward model disappears, leaving a loss on the policy alone.

1. **Model preferences with Bradley-Terry (Eq. 1).**
   - *What it does:* the chance that answer 1 beats answer 2 is a function of the two rewards.
   - *Why it matters:* it gives a way to turn human choices into a loss on a reward.
2. **State the RL goal (Eq. 3).**
   - *What it does:* maximise the expected reward minus $\beta$ times the KL divergence from the reference.
   - *Why it matters:* the KL term keeps the model close to where the reward model is accurate and keeps its answers varied; without it the model collapses onto a few high-reward answers (paper, 3).
3. **Solve it exactly (Eq. 4).**
   - *What it does:* the optimal policy is the reference policy reweighted by $\exp(r/\beta)$ and normalised.
   - *Why it matters:* a closed-form answer means reinforcement learning is not needed to find it, in principle.
4. **Rearrange to express the reward through the policy (Eq. 5).**
   - *What it does:* $r=\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z$.
   - *Why it matters:* the reward is now a function of the policy, apart from a term that depends on the prompt only.
5. **Substitute into Bradley-Terry (Eq. 6).**
   - *What it does:* in the preference probability only the *difference* of the two rewards appears, so the unknown $Z(x)$ cancels.
   - *Why it matters:* this is the key step. The troublesome partition function drops out and the preference probability depends only on the policy and the reference.
6. **Fit it by maximum likelihood (Eq. 7).**
   - *What it does:* take the negative log of that probability over the data set. That is the DPO loss.
   - *Why it matters:* it is a plain binary classification loss on the policy's own log-probabilities.

**One training step in words.** Take a prompt with its preferred and rejected answers. Compute how much more likely the current model finds each answer than the reference model does (a log ratio, times $\beta$). Push the preferred answer's number up and the rejected one's down, giving the larger push to pairs the model currently ranks wrongly (Eq. 8).

## The equations
Equation numbers are the paper's.

**1. The Bradley-Terry preference model** (Eq. 1)

$$
p^{*}(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)\big)+\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y_2)\big)}
$$

**Where:**
- $x$: the prompt; $y_1$, $y_2$: two candidate answers; $y_1\succ y_2$: "$y_1$ is preferred".
- $r^{*}(x,y)$: the unknown true reward of answer $y$ for prompt $x$ (a score for how good people find it).
- $p^{*}$: the chance a person prefers $y_1$.

**Matters:** it links the hidden rewards to the observable choices: the larger the reward gap, the more certain the preference. Only the *difference* of the two rewards matters, which the paper exploits later (the partition function cancels).

**Example (tiny numbers):** $r^{*}(x,y_1)=1$ and $r^{*}(x,y_2)=0$.
- $p^{*}=e^{1}/(e^{1}+e^{0})=2.718/3.718=0.731$.
- Meaning: with a reward gap of 1, people prefer answer 1 about 73% of the time.

**2. The reward-model loss** (Eq. 2)

$$
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi,\mathcal{D})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]
$$

**Where:**
- $r_\phi$: a reward model with parameters $\phi$ to be learned; $\mathcal{D}$: the data set of $(x,y_w,y_l)$.
- $\sigma$: the logistic function, $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$.
- $\mathbb{E}$: the average over the data.

**Matters:** this is the first stage of RLHF: train a classifier so that preferred answers score higher than rejected ones. It is the loss DPO will rewrite in terms of the policy.

**Example (tiny numbers):** $r_\phi(x,y_w)=1$ and $r_\phi(x,y_l)=0$, the right order.
- Loss $=-\ln\sigma(1)=-\ln0.731=0.313$.
- If the model had them reversed ($r_\phi(y_w)=0$, $r_\phi(y_l)=1$): $-\ln\sigma(-1)=1.313$.
- Meaning: ordering the pair correctly gives a small loss; getting it backwards costs four times as much.

**3. The RL objective with a KL leash** (Eq. 3)

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big]
$$

**Where:**
- $\pi_\theta$: the policy being trained; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: the reference policy (the starting model).
- $r_\phi$: the learned reward. $\beta$: a constant. $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$: the Kullback-Leibler divergence, a measure of how different the policy's distribution is from the reference's.

**Matters:** the first term wants high reward; the second punishes drifting from the reference. The paper says the constraint prevents the model from leaving the region where the reward model is accurate, keeps the answers diverse and avoids mode collapse onto single high-reward answers. Without it the policy would chase the learned reward wherever it leads.

**Example (tiny numbers):** $\beta=0.1$. Policy A earns reward 2 and has KL 5; policy B earns reward 3 and has KL 40.
- A: $2-0.1\cdot5=1.5$. B: $3-0.1\cdot40=-1.0$.
- Meaning: B is more rewarded but has drifted much farther from the reference, so the penalty makes it worse.

**4. The optimal policy** (Eq. 4)

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big),\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big)
$$

**Where:**
- $\pi_r$: the best policy for reward $r$ under the KL leash (the exact solution of Eq. 3).
- $Z(x)$: the partition function, the sum that makes the probabilities add up to 1; it depends on the prompt only.
- $\exp(r/\beta)$: boosts answers with high reward; $\beta$ controls how strongly.

**Matters:** it says the optimal policy is just the reference with each answer's probability multiplied by $\exp(r/\beta)$ and renormalised. The catch is that $Z(x)$ is a sum over all possible answers, too expensive to compute, which is why the next step avoids needing it.

**Example (tiny numbers):** two answers with $\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.5,0.5)$ and rewards $(1,0)$.
- With $\beta=1$: $0.5e^{1}=1.359$ and $0.5e^{0}=0.5$, so $Z=1.859$ and $\pi_r=(0.731,\,0.269)$.
- With $\beta=0.1$: $0.5e^{10}=11{,}013$ and $0.5$, so $\pi_r\approx(0.99995,\,0.00005)$.
- Meaning: a small $\beta$ lets the reward dominate and the policy collapses onto the best answer; a large $\beta$ keeps it close to the reference.

**5. The reward written through the policy** (Eq. 5)

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)
$$

**Where:**
- The same symbols as Eq. 4; this is Eq. 4 with the logarithm taken and rearranged.
- $\beta\log\frac{\pi_r}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$: how much more the optimal policy likes $y$ than the reference does, scaled by $\beta$.

**Matters:** it turns the reward into a function of the policy plus a term $\beta\log Z(x)$ that depends on the prompt only, not on the answer. That is the "language model is secretly a reward model" idea.

**Example (tiny numbers):** continuing Eq. 4 with $\beta=1$ ($\pi_r=(0.731,0.269)$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.5,0.5)$, $\ln Z=\ln1.859=0.620$).
- Answer 1: $\ln(0.731/0.5)=0.380$, plus $0.620$ gives $1.0$, the original reward.
- Answer 2: $\ln(0.269/0.5)=-0.620$, plus $0.620$ gives $0.0$.
- Meaning: the two rewards (1 and 0) are recovered exactly from the policy and the reference.

**6. The preference probability in terms of the policy** (Eq. 6)

$$
p^{*}(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\Big)}
$$

**Where:**
- $\pi^{*}$: the optimal policy for the true reward. The rest as before.

**Matters:** put Eq. 5 into Eq. 1. Only the reward *difference* appears and the $\beta\log Z(x)$ terms are equal for both answers, so they cancel. The preference probability now depends only on the policy and the reference.

**Example (tiny numbers):** the same two answers ($\beta=1$): the log ratios are $0.380$ and $-0.620$.
- The exponent is $-0.620-0.380=-1.0$; $p^{*}=1/(1+e^{-1})=0.731$.
- Meaning: the same 0.731 as in Eq. 1, found without ever using $Z$ or the rewards.

**7. The DPO loss** (Eq. 7)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]
$$

**Where:**
- $\pi_\theta$: the policy being trained; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: the frozen reference.
- $y_w$, $y_l$: the preferred and rejected answers; $\beta$: the constant; $\sigma$: the logistic function.
- Each $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is the "implicit reward" of an answer.

**Matters:** training pushes the policy to like the preferred answer more, relative to the reference, than the rejected one. It is the maximum-likelihood loss of Eq. 2, with the reward replaced by the policy's own implicit reward. No reward network, no sampling.

**Example (tiny numbers):** $\pi_\theta(y_w)=0.4$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)=0.2$, $\pi_\theta(y_l)=0.1$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)=0.2$, $\beta=0.1$.
- Log ratios: $\ln2=0.693$ and $\ln0.5=-0.693$; difference $1.386$; times $\beta$: $0.139$.
- $\sigma(0.139)=0.535$; loss $=-\ln0.535=0.626$.
- With $\beta=1$: $\sigma(1.386)=0.8$ and loss $0.223$.
- Meaning: the model already likes the winner more than the loser, so the loss is below the "no information" value of $0.693$; a larger $\beta$ rewards the same gap more.

**8. What the gradient does** (the unnumbered gradient equation in section 4)

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\Big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big]
$$

**Where:**
- $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$: the implicit reward.
- $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$: the weight: large when the model rates the rejected answer above the preferred one.
- The bracket: raise the log-probability of $y_w$ and lower that of $y_l$.

**Matters:** the update does what you would expect (raise the winner, lower the loser), but weighs each pair by how wrongly the model currently ranks it. The paper says a naive version without this weight "can cause the language model to degenerate" (appendix, Table 3).

**Example (tiny numbers):** $\beta=0.1$ and the same ratios as above: $\hat r(y_w)=0.0693$, $\hat r(y_l)=-0.0693$.
- Weight when the model ranks them correctly: $\sigma(-0.0693-0.0693)=\sigma(-0.139)=0.465$.
- If the model had them backwards ($\hat r(y_w)=-0.0693$, $\hat r(y_l)=0.0693$): weight $\sigma(0.139)=0.535$.
- Meaning: the wrongly ranked pair gets the larger push (0.535 against 0.465), though here the difference is small because $\beta$ and the gap are small.

## What they found
DPO fine-tunes language models to match human preferences "as well as or better than existing methods". It exceeds PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generations, and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue, while being "substantially simpler to implement and train" (abstract and paper, 6). The paper tests three tasks (paper, 6): controlled sentiment generation (IMDb movie reviews, GPT-2-large, with preference pairs made by a sentiment classifier), summarization (Reddit TL;DR posts, GPT-J, human preferences gathered by earlier work) and single-turn dialogue (Anthropic Helpful and Harmless, Pythia-2.8B). For the two without a ground-truth reward, answers are scored by the win rate against a baseline, judged by GPT-4 (checked against humans, below).

## Questions I had, answered

**What is the KL divergence term for, and why not just maximise the reward?** The reward model is only accurate near the data it was trained on. The KL term keeps the new model close to the reference so the reward stays trustworthy, keeps its answers varied, and stops mode collapse onto a few high-reward answers (paper, 3). Without it, in my example above, a heavily drifted answer with reward 3 would score worse than a close one with reward 2.

**What does "your language model is secretly a reward model" mean?** The reward of an answer equals $\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ plus a term that depends only on the prompt (Eq. 5). So a trained language model, compared with its reference, already contains a reward function; no separate network is needed (paper, 4 and 5).

**Why can the partition function be ignored?** It depends only on the prompt $x$, not on the answer. Bradley-Terry compares two answers to the same prompt through the *difference* of their rewards, so the term appears in both and cancels (Eq. 6). This is why the intractable sum never has to be computed.

**Does DPO restrict what rewards can be learned?** The paper proves that it does not: under mild assumptions every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) models can be written as $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ for some policy $\pi$ (Theorem 1, section 5.1). It only fixes which member of each equivalence class is used, the one for which the policy is a valid distribution.

**Why is PPO unstable, according to the paper?** In the reinforcement-learning step the objective contains a normalisation term, which can be read as the soft value function of the reference policy. It does not change the optimal solution, but without it "the policy gradient of the objective could have high variance, making learning unstable". PPO handles it with a learned value function, which can be hard to optimise, or with a baseline; DPO's reward needs no baseline (paper, 5.2).

**Does DPO need to sample from the model while training?** No. It trains on a fixed set of preference pairs (offline), where RLHF samples from the model in the training loop. The paper's pipeline: sample answers from the reference for each prompt and label them with human preferences, then minimise the DPO loss (paper, 4).

**What if there is no supervised fine-tuned model?** Then the reference is made by maximising the likelihood of the preferred answers, which also reduces the mismatch between the data and the reference (paper, 4). This was done for the dialogue task.

## Why it mattered
It made preference training much easier to run, so it spread quickly.

## Limits and open questions
The authors list these (paper, 7):
- How does the DPO policy generalise out of distribution, compared with learning from an explicit reward function? The initial result (Table 1) is encouraging but not comprehensive.
- Can self-labelling from the DPO policy make use of unlabelled prompts?
- How does reward over-optimization show up in DPO, and is the slight dip late in the dialogue training (Figure 3, right) an instance of it?
- Scale: only models up to 6 billion parameters were tested; "state-of-the-art models orders of magnitude larger" remain to be explored.
- GPT-4 win rates depend on the prompt; the best way to elicit good judgments from automated systems is open.
- DPO could apply beyond language models and human preferences, for example to generative models of other kinds of data.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What are the steps from the RLHF objective to the DPO loss?
?
1. Model preferences with Bradley-Terry (Eq. 1). 2. State the RL goal: maximise reward minus $\beta$ times the KL from the reference (Eq. 3). 3. Solve it exactly: the optimal policy is the reference reweighted by $\exp(r/\beta)$ (Eq. 4). 4. Rearrange so the reward is a function of the policy (Eq. 5). 5. Put it into Bradley-Terry: only the reward difference appears and the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels (Eq. 6). 6. Fit by maximum likelihood: that is the DPO loss (Eq. 7).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the Bradley-Terry preference model and explain each symbol.
?
$$p^{*}(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)\big)+\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y_2)\big)}$$
- $x$: the prompt; $y_1,y_2$: two answers; $r^{*}$: the unknown true reward; $p^{*}$: the chance a person prefers $y_1$.
- Only the reward difference matters, which DPO uses later (the partition function cancels).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Bradley-Terry example: $r^*(y_1)=1$, $r^*(y_2)=0$. What is the chance $y_1$ is preferred?
?
$e^{1}/(e^{1}+e^{0})=2.718/3.718=0.731$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the reward-model loss of RLHF and explain each symbol.
?
$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi,\mathcal{D})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]$$
- $r_\phi$: the reward model to learn; $\mathcal{D}$: the preference data; $\sigma$: the logistic function; $\mathbb{E}$: average over the data.
- It trains the reward to score preferred answers above rejected ones; DPO rewrites this loss in terms of the policy.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Reward-loss example: $r_\phi(y_w)=1$, $r_\phi(y_l)=0$. What is the loss, and what if they are reversed?
?
$-\ln\sigma(1)=0.313$; reversed, $-\ln\sigma(-1)=1.313$ (my arithmetic): getting the order wrong costs four times as much.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write RLHF's KL-constrained objective and explain each symbol.
?
$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big]$$
- $\pi_\theta$: the policy; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: the reference (starting model); $r_\phi$: the learned reward; $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$: how far the policy's distribution is from the reference's; $\beta$: the strength of the penalty.
- The KL term keeps the model where the reward model is accurate, keeps answers diverse and avoids mode collapse.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm KL objective example: $\beta=0.1$; policy A has reward 2 and KL 5, policy B reward 3 and KL 40. Which scores higher?
?
A: $2-0.1\cdot5=1.5$. B: $3-0.1\cdot40=-1.0$. A wins: B earns more reward but drifted far from the reference (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the optimal policy of the KL-constrained problem and explain each symbol.
?
$$\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big)$$
- $\pi_r$: the best policy for reward $r$; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: the reference; $Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r/\beta)$: the partition function that normalises, depending on the prompt only.
- The reference reweighted by $\exp(r/\beta)$. $Z$ is a sum over all answers, too costly to compute, which DPO avoids.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Optimal-policy example: $\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.5,0.5)$, rewards $(1,0)$. What is $\pi_r$ for $\beta=1$ and $\beta=0.1$?
?
$\beta=1$: $0.5e=1.359$ and $0.5$, $Z=1.859$, $\pi_r=(0.731,0.269)$. $\beta=0.1$: $0.5e^{10}=11{,}013$ and $0.5$, $\pi_r\approx(0.99995,0.00005)$. Small $\beta$ lets the reward dominate; large $\beta$ stays near the reference (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the reward in terms of the policy (Eq. 5) and explain each symbol.
?
$$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$$
- $\beta\log(\pi_r/\pi_{\text{ref}})$: how much more the optimal policy likes $y$ than the reference does; $\beta\log Z(x)$: depends on the prompt only, not on the answer.
- The reward is a function of the policy: "your language model is secretly a reward model".

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Eq. 5 example with $\beta=1$, $\pi_r=(0.731,0.269)$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.5,0.5)$, $\ln Z=0.620$: what are the rewards?
?
Answer 1: $\ln(0.731/0.5)+0.620=0.380+0.620=1.0$. Answer 2: $\ln(0.269/0.5)+0.620=-0.620+0.620=0.0$. The original rewards (1 and 0) are recovered from the policy and the reference (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the preference probability in terms of the policy (Eq. 6) and say why the partition function disappears.
?
$$p^{*}(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\Big)}$$
- $\pi^*$: the optimal policy for the true reward; the rest as before.
- Bradley-Terry uses only the reward difference for the same prompt, and $\beta\log Z(x)$ is the same for both answers, so it cancels. The probability depends only on the policy and the reference.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Eq. 6 example: $\beta=1$, log ratios $0.380$ and $-0.620$. What is the preference probability?
?
Exponent $-0.620-0.380=-1.0$, so $p^*=1/(1+e^{-1})=0.731$, the same as Eq. 1, found without $Z$ or the rewards (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Write the DPO loss and explain each symbol.
?
$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$
- $\pi_\theta$: the policy being trained; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: the frozen reference; $y_w,y_l$: preferred and rejected answers; $\beta$: the constant; each $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is the answer's implicit reward.
- It pushes the policy to like the winner more than the loser, relative to the reference. A plain classification loss: no reward network, no sampling.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm DPO loss example: $\pi_\theta(y_w)=0.4$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)=0.2$, $\pi_\theta(y_l)=0.1$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)=0.2$. What is the loss for $\beta=0.1$ and $\beta=1$?
?
Log ratios $0.693$ and $-0.693$, difference $1.386$. $\beta=0.1$: $\sigma(0.139)=0.535$, loss $0.626$. $\beta=1$: $\sigma(1.386)=0.8$, loss $0.223$. Both below $\ln2=0.693$, the loss for no preference (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm What does the DPO gradient do, and what is the weight for?
?
$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l)\big)\Big]$$
- $\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$: the implicit reward. The weight is large when the model rates the rejected answer above the preferred one.
- It raises the winner's probability and lowers the loser's, with a bigger push for wrongly ranked pairs. Without the weight the model can degenerate (appendix Table 3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-llm Gradient weight example: $\beta=0.1$, $\hat r(y_w)=0.0693$, $\hat r(y_l)=-0.0693$. What is the weight, and if the ranking were backwards?
?
Correct ranking: $\sigma(-0.139)=0.465$. Backwards: $\sigma(0.139)=0.535$. The wrongly ranked pair gets the larger push (my arithmetic).
