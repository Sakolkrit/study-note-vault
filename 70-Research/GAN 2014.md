# GAN 2014

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** Two networks play a game. A forger tries to make fake pictures, and a detective tries to spot the fakes. Each gets better because of the other, until the detective can only guess.

Paper: "Generative Adversarial Nets", arXiv 1406.2661, version 1 of 10 June 2014, 9 pages. Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my own arithmetic, done in Python. This paper's arXiv licence is not open (the usual arXiv licence), so its figures are not copied; a free redrawing is used instead.

## The problem
The promise of deep learning is to learn rich models of "probability distributions over the kinds of data encountered in artificial intelligence applications, such as natural images, audio waveforms containing speech, and symbols in natural language corpora" (paper, 1). The big successes so far were discriminative: map an input to a label. Deep generative models, which learn to produce new data, "have had less of an impact", because of "the difficulty of approximating many intractable probabilistic computations that arise in maximum likelihood estimation", and because it was hard to use the well-behaved piecewise linear units in them (paper, 1). See [[Generative Models]].

## What people did before
- **Undirected models with latent variables** such as restricted Boltzmann machines and deep Boltzmann machines: they need a "partition function" (a sum over all states) that is intractable, estimated by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), where "mixing poses a significant problem" (paper, 2).
- **Deep belief networks:** hybrids with the difficulties of both directed and undirected models (paper, 2).
- **Score matching and noise-contrastive estimation:** do not bound the likelihood, but need the density up to a normalising constant; noise-contrastive estimation uses a fixed noise distribution, so "learning slows dramatically" once the model is roughly right (paper, 2).
- **Generative machines trained by backpropagation:** generative stochastic networks (which need a Markov chain to sample), variational auto-encoders and stochastic backpropagation (paper, 2).

## The idea
Do not write down a probability formula at all. Instead put the generative model in a game against an adversary, "a discriminative model that learns to determine whether a sample is from the model distribution or the data distribution" (paper, 1). The generator is like "a team of counterfeiters" making fake currency and the discriminator is like "the police, trying to detect the counterfeit currency"; competition "drives both teams to improve their methods until the counterfeits are indistinguishable from the genuine articles" (paper, 1). In the paper's special case both are multilayer perceptrons, so both train by [[Backpropagation]] and [[Dropout]], and samples come from a single forward pass: "No approximate inference or Markov chains are necessary" (paper, 1). See [[GAN]].

## The architecture, part by part

The two discriminator boxes are the same network. Figure: Mtanti, from [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Generative_Adversarial_Network_illustration.svg), CC BY-SA 4.0. It is a redrawing of the idea, not the paper's Figure 1: the paper's Figure 1 instead shows, on a one-dimensional example, how the data distribution, the generator's distribution and the discriminator change over training (described below).*

The paper has no big architecture figure; the model is two small networks and a game. The parts (paper, 3 and 5):
- **The noise $z$ and its prior $p_z(z)$.**
  - *What it does:* random numbers (in the paper's Figure 1, uniform ones) fed into the generator. The paper puts noise "as the input to only the bottommost layer of the generator network" (paper, 5).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the only source of variety. Different noise gives different samples; with no noise the generator would output one picture.
- **The generator $G(z;\theta_g)$.**
  - *What it does:* "a differentiable function represented by a multilayer perceptron" mapping noise to data space; it implicitly defines the distribution $p_g$ of its samples (paper, 3 and 4). In the experiments it uses a mix of rectified linear and sigmoid activations (paper, 5).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the forger. It never sees a real image; it learns only from the gradient that flows back through the discriminator (paper, 6).
- **The discriminator $D(x;\theta_d)$.**
  - *What it does:* a second multilayer perceptron that outputs one number between 0 and 1: the probability that $x$ came from the data rather than from $G$ (paper, 3). It uses maxout activations and dropout during training (paper, 5).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the detective and also the teacher: its mistakes tell the generator in which direction to move.
- **The minimax game.**
  - *What it does:* $D$ is trained to label real and fake correctly; $G$ is trained to make $D$ wrong (equation 1 below).
  - *Why it matters:* it replaces a likelihood formula with a classifier, which is what deep learning is good at.
- **The training loop (Algorithm 1).**
  - *What it does:* alternates $k$ steps for $D$ and one step for $G$; the paper uses $k=1$ (paper, Algorithm 1).
  - *Why it matters:* it keeps $D$ near its best given the current $G$, "so long as $G$ changes slowly enough" (paper, 3).

## The core mechanism, step by step

**One training round (paper, Algorithm 1; my numbered paraphrase).**
1. **Sample noise:** draw $m$ random noise vectors $z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(m)}$.
2. **Sample real data:** draw $m$ real examples $x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)}$ from the training set.
3. **Update $D$:** change $D$'s weights to push $D(x^{(i)})$ toward 1 and $D(G(z^{(i)}))$ toward 0 (equation 5 below). Do this $k$ times (the paper uses $k=1$).
4. **Sample new noise:** draw another $m$ noise vectors.
5. **Update $G$:** change $G$'s weights so that $D$ is more likely to call $G(z)$ real (equations 1 and 4). Gradients flow through $D$ into $G$; $D$'s weights are not changed in this step.
6. **Repeat.** The updates "can use any standard gradient-based learning rule"; the paper used momentum (paper, 4).

**What the paper's Figure 1 shows (read from its caption; the figure is not reproduced).** A one-dimensional example. The black dotted line is the data distribution, the green solid line is the generator's distribution $p_g$, and the blue dashed line is the discriminator. (a) Near convergence, $p_g$ is close to $p_\text{data}$ and $D$ is a partially accurate classifier. (b) $D$ is trained to tell the two apart and converges to $D^*(x)=p_\text{data}(x)/(p_\text{data}(x)+p_g(x))$. (c) After a $G$ update, the gradient of $D$ has guided $G(z)$ to flow toward regions more likely to be called data. (d) After several steps, if the networks have enough capacity, $p_g=p_\text{data}$ and $D(x)=\frac12$ everywhere.

**Why the alternation (paper, 3).** "Optimizing D to completion in the inner loop of training is computationally prohibitive, and on finite datasets would result in overfitting." So $D$ gets $k$ steps and $G$ gets one.

## The equations

**1. The minimax value function** (paper, Eq. 1)

$$
\min_G\max_D V(D,G)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\text{data}(x)}\big[\log D(x)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z(z)}\big[\log\big(1-D(G(z))\big)\big]
$$

**Where:**
- $G$: the generator; $D$: the discriminator; $V(D,G)$: the value of the game, which $D$ wants high and $G$ wants low.
- $x\sim p_\text{data}$: a real example drawn from the training data; $D(x)$: $D$'s probability that it is real.
- $z\sim p_z$: noise; $G(z)$: a fake example; $1-D(G(z))$: $D$'s probability that the fake is fake.
- $\log$: the natural logarithm; it is $0$ for a perfect probability of 1 and very negative for a probability near 0.

**Matters:** the first term rewards $D$ for calling real data real, the second rewards $D$ for calling fakes fake; $G$ can only change the second term, and tries to make it as bad as possible for $D$. Without the logarithms the game would not be a likelihood of the correct labels: the paper notes that the training objective for $D$ "can be interpreted as maximizing the log-likelihood for estimating the conditional probability $P(Y=y\mid x)$", where $y=1$ means real (paper, 4.1).

**Example (tiny numbers):** a discriminator that gives $D(x)=0.9$ on a real example and $D(G(z))=0.2$ on a fake.
- $V=\ln0.9+\ln(1-0.2)=-0.105-0.223=-0.329$.
- A perfect detective ($D(x)=1$, $D(G(z))=0$) has $V=0$, the highest value possible.
- A detective who can only guess ($D=0.5$ everywhere) has $V=2\ln0.5=-1.386=-\ln4$.
- Meaning: the closer $V$ is to 0, the better the detective; the forger wants it pushed down to $-\ln4$.

**2. The best discriminator for a fixed generator** (paper, Eq. 2, Proposition 1)

$$
D_G^*(x)=\frac{p_\text{data}(x)}{p_\text{data}(x)+p_g(x)}
$$

**Where:**
- $p_\text{data}(x)$: how common the example $x$ is among real data.
- $p_g(x)$: how common $x$ is among the generator's samples.
- $D_G^*(x)$: the best possible discriminator for this fixed $G$ (the optimum).

**Matters:** it says the detective's best answer is just "the share of real among all samples that look like $x$". The proof maximises $a\log y+b\log(1-y)$ for each $x$, whose maximum is at $y=a/(a+b)$ (paper, 4.1). It gives a clean target for what $D$ learns, and putting it back into equation 1 gives the criterion for $G$ (next equation).

**Example (tiny numbers):** at one point $x$, real data has density $0.6$ and the generator $0.2$.
- $D^*=0.6/(0.6+0.2)=0.75$.
- If the generator is perfect ($p_g=p_\text{data}=0.3$ at that point): $D^*=0.3/0.6=0.5$.
- Meaning: where real data is three times as common, the best guess is 75% real; when the generator matches the data the best guess is a coin flip.

**3. What the generator is really minimising** (paper, Eqs. 4 to 6, Theorem 1)

$$
C(G)=\max_D V(G,D)=-\log4+2\cdot\mathrm{JSD}\big(p_\text{data}\,\|\,p_g\big)
$$

**Where:**
- $C(G)$: the value of the game when $D$ is the best possible discriminator for $G$ (Eq. 4: $\mathbb{E}_{p_\text{data}}[\log D_G^*]+\mathbb{E}_{p_g}[\log(1-D_G^*)]$).
- $\mathrm{JSD}$: the Jensen-Shannon divergence, which is $\frac12\mathrm{KL}(p_\text{data}\|m)+\frac12\mathrm{KL}(p_g\|m)$ with $m=\frac{p_\text{data}+p_g}{2}$ (Eq. 5 is the same thing written with two Kullback-Leibler terms). It is never negative and is zero only when the two distributions are equal.
- $-\log4$: the lowest possible value, reached only when $p_g=p_\text{data}$.

**Matters:** it proves the game has exactly one winning point: the generator copies the data distribution. Without this result the game would just be an engineering trick; with it there is a guarantee, in the idealised case of unlimited capacity (the paper says this is in "a non-parametric setting", paper, 4). See [[KL Divergence]].

**Example (tiny numbers):** two possible pictures. Real data: $(0.5,0.5)$.
- Perfect generator $(0.5,0.5)$: JSD $=0$, so $C=-\ln4=-1.386$.
- Lopsided generator $(0.9,0.1)$: JSD $=0.1017$, so $C=-1.386+0.2034=-1.183$ (and Eq. 4 computed directly also gives $-1.183$).
- Slightly lopsided $(0.7,0.3)$: JSD $=0.021$, so $C=-1.344$.
- Meaning: the worse the copy, the higher $C$; the generator wants to bring it down to $-1.386$.

**4. The generator's practical objective** (paper, section 3, in the text)

$$
\max_G\ \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z(z)}\big[\log D(G(z))\big]\quad\text{instead of}\quad\min_G\ \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z(z)}\big[\log\big(1-D(G(z))\big)\big]
$$

**Where:**
- $D(G(z))$: $D$'s probability that a fake is real.
- The two forms are the "same fixed point of the dynamics of G and D", but the right-hand one "saturates": early on, $D$ rejects fakes with high confidence, so $D(G(z))$ is near 0 and $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ is flat (paper, 3).

**Matters:** at the start $G$ is poor and $D$ easily spots it. The original objective then gives almost no gradient, so $G$ cannot learn, while the swapped one "provides much stronger gradients early in learning" (paper, 3). Without the change training can stall right at the start.

**Example (tiny numbers):** write $D=\sigma(a)$ and look at the slope with respect to the score $a$ (my calculation).
- $D(G(z))=0.01$: slope of $\log(1-D)$ is $-0.01$; slope of $\log D$ is $0.99$, which is 99 times larger.
- $D(G(z))=0.1$: $-0.1$ against $0.9$ (9 times).
- $D(G(z))=0.5$: $-0.5$ against $0.5$ (equal).
- Meaning: the swap matters most exactly when $G$ is losing badly, and makes no difference once $G$ is fooling $D$.

**5. The two updates of Algorithm 1** (paper, Algorithm 1)

$$
\nabla_{\theta_d}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\log D\big(x^{(i)}\big)+\log\Big(1-D\big(G\big(z^{(i)}\big)\big)\Big)\Big]\ \text{(ascend)},\qquad
\nabla_{\theta_g}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\Big(1-D\big(G\big(z^{(i)}\big)\big)\Big)\ \text{(descend)}
$$

**Where:**
- $m$: the size of the minibatch; $x^{(i)}$: a real example; $z^{(i)}$: a noise vector.
- $\theta_d$, $\theta_g$: the weights of $D$ and $G$.
- $\nabla$: the gradient (the direction of fastest change). "Ascend" means move to increase the number; "descend" to decrease it.

**Matters:** it is equation 1 estimated on a small sample, so each step is cheap. $D$ climbs the objective and $G$ descends it. (The paper's Algorithm 1 prints the generator update in the original form; its text in section 3 gives the swapped form of equation 4 as the practical alternative.)

**Example (tiny numbers):** a minibatch of two with $D(x)=(0.9,0.8)$ and $D(G(z))=(0.2,0.3)$.
- $D$'s objective: $\tfrac12[\ln0.9+\ln0.8+\ln0.8+\ln0.7]=-0.454$ (in the sum over the four terms, divided by $m=2$).
- $G$'s original loss: $\tfrac12[\ln0.8+\ln0.7]=-0.290$. With the swapped objective the loss is $-\tfrac12[\ln0.2+\ln0.3]=1.407$.
- Meaning: $D$ wants its number to rise toward 0; $G$ wants to lower its own.

## What they found
The paper trains adversarial nets on MNIST (handwritten digits), the Toronto Face Database (TFD) and CIFAR-10 (paper, 5). It claims "the potential of the framework through qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the generated samples" (abstract), not a win.

What each column is, and why it matters:
- **Model:** DBN is a deep belief network, Stacked CAE a stack of contractive auto-encoders, Deep GSN a deep generative stochastic network (all earlier generative models from the paper's references).
- **MNIST and TFD:** the average log-likelihood of the test data, estimated by fitting a Gaussian Parzen window to samples from the model (a kernel density estimate) and the width $\sigma$ chosen by cross-validation (paper, 5). A GAN has no explicit likelihood, so this indirect method is the only way to compare it with the others. On MNIST the real-valued version of the data set is used; on TFD the error is computed across folds.
- **± values:** the standard error: across test examples for MNIST and across folds for TFD (paper's caption).

**Summary: what the table shows.**
- **MNIST:** adversarial nets are first, 225 against Deep GSN's 214 (11 higher, mine), a gap of nearly 5 combined standard errors (mine: $\sqrt{2^2+1.1^2}=2.3$, and $11/2.3=4.8$).
- **TFD:** Stacked CAE is first with 2110 and adversarial nets second with 2057, 53 lower (mine), which is within about one combined standard error (mine: $\sqrt{26^2+50^2}=56$). The GAN is above DBN (1909) and Deep GSN (1890).
- **Caution:** the paper itself says "this method of estimating the likelihood has somewhat high variance and does not perform well in high dimensional spaces but it is the best method available to our knowledge", and makes "no claim that these samples are better than samples generated by existing methods" (paper, 5). So the table is weak evidence.

**Table 2: challenges in generative modelling** (paper's Table 2; one cell per approach and operation).

| Operation | Deep directed graphical models | Deep undirected graphical models | Generative autoencoders | Adversarial models |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Training | Inference needed during training | Inference needed during training; MCMC needed to approximate the partition function gradient | Enforced tradeoff between mixing and power of reconstruction generation | Synchronizing the discriminator with the generator; "Helvetica" |
| Inference | Learned approximate inference | Variational inference | MCMC-based inference | Learned approximate inference |
| Sampling | No difficulties | Requires Markov chain | Requires Markov chain | No difficulties |
| Evaluating $p(x)$ | Intractable, may be approximated with AIS | Intractable, may be approximated with AIS | Not explicitly represented, may be approximated with Parzen density estimation | Not explicitly represented, may be approximated with Parzen density estimation |
| Model design | Nearly all models incur extreme difficulty | Careful design needed to ensure multiple properties | Any differentiable function is theoretically permitted | Any differentiable function is theoretically permitted |

Written by me, an AI, from the paper's table, checking the layout against the page picture; wording shortened only slightly.

What each column and row is, and why it matters:
- **Rows:** the five jobs a generative model must do: be trained, infer hidden variables, produce samples, give a probability for a datum, and be designed. A model is useful only if none of them is a roadblock.
- **Columns:** four families, the three older ones and the adversarial one.
- **AIS:** annealed importance sampling, a method to approximate an intractable probability (general knowledge). "Helvetica" is the paper's name for the failure where $G$ is trained too much without updating $D$ and "collapses too many values of $z$ to the same value of $x$" (paper, 6), now usually called mode collapse (general knowledge).

**Summary: what the table shows.**
- **Where adversarial models win:** sampling (no Markov chain) and model design (any differentiable function is theoretically allowed); inference is learned and approximate, not needed during training.
- **Where they lose:** they have no explicit probability of $x$ (evaluated only by Parzen estimates), and training needs $D$ and $G$ "synchronized".
- **Caution:** this table is the authors' own summary, not a measurement; it describes difficulty qualitatively.

## Questions I had, answered

**Why is it called "adversarial"?** The two networks have opposite goals on the same number $V$: $D$ wants it high and $G$ wants it low (equation 1). Each one's progress is the other's problem.

**What does it mean that the solution is $D=\frac12$ everywhere?** When the generator's samples are exactly like real data, the best the detective can do is guess, so it says 0.5 for everything (paper, 4.1; example under equation 2).

**Why train $D$ for only $k=1$ step, and not to the end?** Training $D$ fully each time is too expensive and "would result in overfitting" on a finite data set; the paper keeps $D$ near its best by alternating, so long as $G$ moves slowly (paper, 3).

**If $G$ never sees real images, how does it learn?** Only through the gradient that flows back from $D$ into $G$. The paper adds a statistical bonus from this: "components of the input are not copied directly into the generator's parameters" (paper, 6).

**What is the "Helvetica scenario"?** If $G$ is trained too much without updating $D$, it can collapse many different $z$ to the same $x$ and lose diversity (paper, 6). Today it is called mode collapse (general knowledge).

**Is the GAN proof valid for the networks actually used?** Only in the ideal case. The theory assumes unlimited capacity ("non-parametric limit") and a $D$ trained to its optimum at each step; with multilayer perceptrons "there are multiple critical points in parameter space", and the authors only say that the good practical results of such networks suggest they are a reasonable model despite "their lack of theoretical guarantees" (paper, 4.2).

## Why it mattered
It gave a new way to train a generative model with nothing but backpropagation: no Markov chains and no inference during learning (paper, 6). It also showed the idea of training a model against a learned judge. Sharp image generation and many later variants followed (from memory).

## Limits and open questions
The authors list these (paper, 6 and 7):
- No explicit representation of $p_g(x)$, so likelihood can only be estimated indirectly.
- $D$ must be well synchronised with $G$, or the generator collapses (the Helvetica scenario).
- The Parzen estimate has high variance and works badly in high dimensions, so how to evaluate such models is an open question.
- Extensions: a conditional model $p(x\mid c)$ by adding $c$ as an input to both networks, learned inference by an auxiliary network, modelling all conditionals, semi-supervised learning from the discriminator's features, and faster training by better coordination of $G$ and $D$ (paper, 7).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Why does the value function use logarithms?
?
$\log D(x)$ and $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ are the log-likelihoods of the right labels (real is 1, fake is 0), so maximising $V$ over $D$ is training a classifier. A wrong confident answer is punished very hard ($\ln0.01=-4.6$ against $\ln0.9=-0.105$), and the optimum can be solved exactly, giving $D^*=p_\text{data}/(p_\text{data}+p_g)$.

?
$D$'s correct label for a fake is "not real", whose probability is $1-D(G(z))$. The sum of the two log-probabilities is then the log-likelihood of the correct labels on both kinds of data. The generator may use $\log D(G(z))$ instead as a practical trick (equation 4).

$$D_G^*(x)=\frac{p_\text{data}(x)}{p_\text{data}(x)+p_g(x)}$$
- $p_\text{data}(x)$: density of real data at $x$; $p_g(x)$: density of the generator's samples at $x$; $D_G^*$: the best possible discriminator for a fixed $G$.
- The best guess is the share of real among all samples that look like $x$; it equals $\frac12$ when $p_g=p_\text{data}$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Optimal-discriminator example: real density $0.6$, generator density $0.2$. What is $D^*$, and what if the generator is perfect?
?
$D^*=0.6/0.8=0.75$. Perfect generator ($p_g=p_\text{data}=0.3$): $0.3/0.6=0.5$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Why does the optimal discriminator have this form (what is the proof idea)?
?
For each point $x$ the objective is $a\log y+b\log(1-y)$ with $a=p_\text{data}(x)$ and $b=p_g(x)$. Its maximum over $y$ in $[0,1]$ is at $y=a/(a+b)$ (the paper, 4.1). With $a=0.6$, $b=0.2$ that is $0.75$. The discriminator does not need to be defined outside the supports of the two distributions.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Write the generator's criterion once $D$ is optimal (Eq. 6) and explain each symbol.
?
$$C(G)=\max_D V(G,D)=-\log4+2\cdot\mathrm{JSD}\big(p_\text{data}\,\|\,p_g\big)$$
- $C(G)$: the game's value when $D$ is the best for $G$; $\mathrm{JSD}$: Jensen-Shannon divergence, $\ge0$ and $0$ only if the distributions are equal; $-\log4$: the lowest value of $C$.
- The unique minimum is at $p_g=p_\text{data}$, so the game's only winner is a perfect copy (Theorem 1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Criterion example: real data $(0.5,0.5)$ over two pictures. What is $C(G)$ for $p_g=(0.5,0.5)$, $(0.7,0.3)$ and $(0.9,0.1)$?
?
$C=-1.386$ (JSD 0), $-1.344$ (JSD 0.021) and $-1.183$ (JSD 0.1017), by $-\ln4+2\,\mathrm{JSD}$; direct use of Eq. 4 gives the same (my arithmetic). The worse the copy, the higher $C$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Why does the value $-\log4$ appear in the criterion?
?
When $p_g=p_\text{data}$ the best discriminator says $\frac12$ everywhere, so $V=\ln\frac12+\ln\frac12=-\ln4=-1.386$. The divergence term is then $0$. It is the value at which neither network can improve.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Why does a Jensen-Shannon divergence appear, and why the factor $2$?
?
Putting $D^*$ into $V$ gives two expectations of the form $\log\frac{p}{p+q}$. Subtracting $-\log4$ turns them into two KL divergences to the mixture $m=\frac{p_\text{data}+p_g}{2}$ (Eq. 5), and their sum is $2\cdot\mathrm{JSD}$ (Eq. 6) because JSD is defined with a $\frac12$ in front of each. Example: JSD $0.1017$ becomes $0.2034$ added to $-1.386$.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Write the generator's practical objective (section 3) and say why it replaces the original.
?
$$\max_G\ \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}\big[\log D(G(z))\big]\quad\text{instead of}\quad\min_G\ \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}\big[\log\big(1-D(G(z))\big)\big]$$
- $D(G(z))$: the probability the discriminator gives to a fake being real.
- Same fixed point, but early on $D$ rejects fakes confidently, so $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ saturates and gives little gradient; the swapped form gives strong gradients early in learning.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Gradient example: $D(G(z))=0.01$, $0.1$ and $0.5$. How do the slopes of $\log(1-D)$ and $\log D$ compare?
?
Slope with respect to the score: $-0.01$ against $0.99$ (99 times), $-0.1$ against $0.9$ (9 times), $-0.5$ against $0.5$ (equal) (my arithmetic). The swap matters most when the generator is losing.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Why is $\log D(G(z))$ better than $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ at the start of training?
?
At the start $D(G(z))$ is near $0$ and $\log(1-D)$ is nearly flat there: its slope is only $-D=-0.01$ at $D=0.01$, so the generator learns almost nothing. The slope of $\log D$ is $1-D=0.99$. Without the swap, training can stall before it starts.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Write the two minibatch updates of Algorithm 1 and explain each symbol.
?
$$\nabla_{\theta_d}\tfrac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\log D\big(x^{(i)}\big)+\log\Big(1-D\big(G\big(z^{(i)}\big)\big)\Big)\Big]\ \text{(ascend)},\quad
\nabla_{\theta_g}\tfrac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\Big(1-D\big(G\big(z^{(i)}\big)\big)\Big)\ \text{(descend)}$$
- $m$: minibatch size; $x^{(i)}$: real examples; $z^{(i)}$: noise; $\theta_d,\theta_g$: the weights of $D$ and $G$; ascend raises the value, descend lowers it.
- It is equation 1 estimated on a small sample: $D$ climbs it and $G$ descends it. The text suggests the swapped form as a practical alternative.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Minibatch example: $D(x)=(0.9,0.8)$, $D(G(z))=(0.2,0.3)$. What are $D$'s objective and $G$'s two losses?
?
$D$: $\tfrac12[\ln0.9+\ln0.8+\ln0.8+\ln0.7]=-0.454$. $G$ original: $\tfrac12[\ln0.8+\ln0.7]=-0.290$; swapped: $-\tfrac12[\ln0.2+\ln0.3]=1.407$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-generative Why average over a minibatch of $m$ and divide by $m$?
?
The expectations in equation 1 cannot be computed exactly, so they are estimated from $m$ samples. Dividing by $m$ keeps the size of the gradient the same whatever $m$ is, so the learning rate need not change; with $m=2$ in the example the objective is the sum of four logs divided by 2, which is $-0.454$.

With multilayer perceptrons there are many critical points, and the authors only argue that their good practical performance makes them a reasonable model despite the lack of guarantees.
