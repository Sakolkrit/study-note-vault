# CLIP 2021

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** Show a computer 400 million pictures that each come with a caption, and teach it to match each picture to its own caption. Then you can ask it "which of these sentences fits this new picture?", and it can answer about things nobody ever labelled for it.

Paper: "Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision", arXiv 2103.00020, first posted 26 February 2021, 48 pages. Concept note: [[CLIP]]. Marks: "(paper)" or "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my own arithmetic, done in Python. This paper's arXiv licence is not open (the usual arXiv licence), so its own figures are not copied; the diagram used is OpenAI's own copy of the same picture, released under the MIT licence (see the caption).

## The problem
Image classifiers of the time were trained to pick from a fixed list of categories (for example the 1000 ImageNet classes). The abstract says that this limits their generality, because more labelled data is needed to specify any other visual concept. Could a model learn visual ideas directly from the huge amount of text that already sits next to pictures on the internet, as language models had done with text alone? "Could scalable pre-training methods which learn directly from web text result in a similar breakthrough in computer vision?" (paper, 1).

## What people did before
- **Supervised pre-training on labelled sets:** train on [[Image Classification]] data such as ImageNet, then reuse the model. The paper notes that this was still standard practice in vision (paper).
- **Weakly supervised pre-training:** predicting hashtags on Instagram images (Mahajan et al., 2018) or the noisy classes of the JFT-300M dataset (used by BiT and [[ViT 2020]]). The paper says both designed and limited their supervision to 1000 and 18,291 classes, and used a fixed [[Softmax]] classifier, which limits zero-shot use (paper).
- **Learning from text paired with images:** the paper lists earlier work, from 1999 on. Visual N-Grams (Li et al., 2017) reached 11.5% zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet, which the paper calls well below the 88.4% state of the art of the time (paper). VirTex, ICMLM and ConVIRT showed the potential of transformers, masked language modelling and contrastive goals, but trained for "accelerator days" on one to two hundred thousand images (paper).
- **Predicting the caption:** the authors' own first attempt, a CNN plus a text [[Transformer]] trained to predict each caption word by word, learned too slowly (see "What they found").

## The idea
Do not ask the model to write the exact caption. Only ask which text goes with which image in a batch. In a batch of $N$ pairs it must pick the right one out of $N\times N$ possible pairings (paper). This is [[Contrastive Learning]]. An image encoder and a text encoder map into one shared [[Embedding]] space, and training makes the [[Cosine Similarity]] high for the $N$ real pairs and low for the $N^2-N$ wrong ones, with a symmetric [[Cross-Entropy Loss]] (paper). The paper credits the batch construction to the N-pair loss (Sohn, 2016), popularised as InfoNCE by Oord et al. At test time, to classify an image, write the class names as sentences and pick the sentence closest to the image: zero-shot transfer, steered by wording, which is a form of [[Prompt Engineering]].

## The architecture, part by part

![[clip-contrastive-pretraining.png]]

Figure: OpenAI, from [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Contrastive_Language-Image_Pretraining.png), taken from the CLIP repository on GitHub and released under the MIT licence (the licence given on the Commons page). I compared it by eye with the paper's own Figure 1 on page 2: it shows the same three panels with the same content, so it is, as far as I can tell, the paper's figure as published by its authors under an open licence. The paper's caption says that the learned text encoder "synthesizes a zero-shot linear classifier by embedding the names or descriptions of the target dataset's classes".*

CLIP is two encoders, a projection and a score. Its parts (paper, 2.3 to 2.5):
- **The dataset WIT (WebImageText).**
  - *What it does:* 400 million (image, text) pairs from public internet sources, found by searching for texts that contain one of 500,000 queries (words with high pointwise mutual information, Wikipedia article names and all WordNet synsets), with up to 20,000 pairs per query to balance the classes. Its total word count is similar to WebText, the data of GPT-2 (paper, 2.2).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the supervision. Natural language names many more concepts than a list of 1000 classes; the data is noisy but huge.
- **The image encoder.**
  - *What it does:* either a modified ResNet-50 (ResNet-D changes, antialiased blur pooling, and the global average pooling replaced by one layer of "transformer-style" multi-head attention pooling) or a Vision Transformer that follows [[ViT 2020]] with an extra layer normalisation before the transformer (paper, 2.4). See [[ResNet 2015]] and [[Vision Transformer]].
  - *Why it matters:* it turns a picture into one vector. Two families were tried so that the result does not depend on one design.
- **The text encoder.**
  - *What it does:* a [[Transformer]] with the changes of GPT-2: 12 layers, 512 wide, 8 attention heads, 63 million parameters, working on lower-cased byte pair encoding with a vocabulary of 49,152 words (the hyperparameter table says 49,408, see the limits), at most 76 tokens, with the sequence wrapped in [SOS] and [EOS]; the top-layer activation at [EOS] is the text feature, which is layer-normalised and linearly projected (paper, 2.4). It uses masked self-attention.
  - *Why it matters:* it turns a sentence into one vector in the same space. The masked attention keeps open the option of starting from a pre-trained language model (not done in the paper).
- **The linear projections $W_i$ and $W_t$.**
  - *What it does:* a single linear map from each encoder's output into the shared embedding space; there is no non-linear projection head (paper, 2.3).
  - *Why it matters:* it makes the two kinds of feature the same size, so they can be compared. The authors saw "no difference in training efficiency" with a non-linear head.
- **The temperature $\tau$.**
  - *What it does:* a learned scalar that stretches the cosine similarities before the softmax. It starts at the equivalent of 0.07 and is clipped so the logits are never scaled by more than 100 (paper, 2.5).
  - *Why it matters:* cosine similarities lie between $-1$ and $1$, so without a scale the softmax would be nearly flat; the scale decides how sharp the model's confidence is (equation 1 and example).
- **Training simplifications.**
  - *What it does:* trained from scratch (no ImageNet weights, no pre-trained text weights); the only image augmentation is a random square crop; the text is not sub-sampled to one sentence (paper, 2.3).
  - *Why it matters:* with 400 million pairs "over-fitting is not a major concern", so the recipe can be much simpler than ConVIRT's.
- **The zero-shot classifier.**
  - *What it does:* a multinomial logistic regression with L2-normalised inputs and weights, no bias and temperature scaling, whose weights are generated by the text encoder from the class names (paper, 3.1.2). The text encoder is thus a "hypernetwork" that writes a linear classifier.
  - *Why it matters:* a new classification task needs no training, only a list of sentences. The classifier is computed once and cached, so its cost is spread over all predictions.

The largest ResNet (RN50x64) took 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs, about 29 GPU-years; the largest ViT took 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs, about 8.4 GPU-years (paper, 2.5; my arithmetic). The paper's comparison: Mahajan and others needed 19 GPU-years, and Xie and others 33 TPUv3 core-years (paper, 2.3).

## The core mechanism, step by step

**One training step (my numbered paraphrase of paper, 2.3 and Figure 3).**
1. **Take a batch** of $N=32{,}768$ (image, text) pairs; image $i$ and text $i$ belong together.
2. **Encode:** the image encoder gives a feature vector for each image, the text encoder one for each text.
3. **Project and normalise:** map each into the shared space with $W_i$ or $W_t$ and scale it to length 1 (an L2 normalisation), so that dot products are cosine similarities.
4. **Compare everything with everything:** the $N\times N$ table of cosine similarities, each multiplied by the learned scale $e^{t}$ (equation 1).
5. **Score the rows and the columns:** treat each row as a classification of "which text belongs to image $i$", each column as "which image belongs to text $j$"; the right answer is the diagonal.
6. **Average the two cross-entropy losses** (equation 2) and update both encoders and the temperature.
7. **Repeat** for 32 epochs.

**How zero-shot classification works (paper, 3.1.2).**
1. Write each class name into a sentence, for example "A photo of a {label}."
2. Encode all the sentences with the text encoder once, and keep them as a classifier.
3. Encode the image, compute its cosine similarity with every sentence, scale, and apply softmax (equation 3).
4. The most probable sentence is the prediction.

Prompt wording matters: for Oxford-IIIT Pets "A photo of a {label}, a type of pet." helped; for OCR datasets quotes around the text helped; for satellite images "a satellite photo of a {label}." helped (paper, 3.1.4). The paper also averages many prompts in embedding space (equation 4).

## The equations
The paper's pseudocode (Figure 3) and text give the following. Equation 4 is my formulation of what the text describes in words.

**1. Scaled cosine similarities** (paper, Figure 3 and 2.3)

$$
\mathrm{logits}_{ij}=e^{t}\,\frac{\mathbf{I}_i\cdot\mathbf{T}_j}{\lVert\mathbf{I}_i\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{T}_j\rVert}=e^{t}\,\mathbf{I}_{e,i}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{e,j}
$$

**Where:**
- $\mathbf{I}_i=W_i^{\top}f_\text{image}(\text{image } i)$ and $\mathbf{T}_j=W_t^{\top}f_\text{text}(\text{text } j)$: the projected image and text vectors; $\mathbf{I}_{e,i}$ and $\mathbf{T}_{e,j}$ are the same vectors scaled to length 1.
- $\mathbf{I}_{e,i}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{e,j}$: their dot product, which is their cosine similarity, between $-1$ and $1$.
- $t$: the learned log-temperature; $e^{t}=1/\tau$ is the scale. It starts at $1/0.07=14.3$ and is clipped at 100.
- $\mathrm{logits}_{ij}$: the entry in row $i$ (image) and column $j$ (text) of the $N\times N$ table.

**Matters:** normalising makes only the direction of a vector matter, not its length. The scale decides how peaked the softmax is: with cosines in $[-1,1]$, a scale of 1 would make all $N$ pairings look almost equally likely, and a scale of 100 makes a small gap decisive. The paper learns it "to avoid turning it as a hyper-parameter" and clips it because without the clip training was unstable (paper, 2.3 and 2.5).

**Example (tiny numbers):** one image and three texts with cosines $0.31,\ 0.24,\ 0.20$ (the first is the right match).
- Scale 1: softmax probabilities $0.354,\ 0.330,\ 0.317$ (almost a three-way tie).
- Scale $14.3$ (the starting value): $0.635,\ 0.233,\ 0.132$.
- Scale 100 (the clip): $0.999,\ 0.001,\ 0.000$.
- Meaning: the same similarities become sure or unsure depending on the scale.

**2. The symmetric contrastive loss** (paper, Figure 3: `loss_i`, `loss_t`, `loss = (loss_i + loss_t)/2`)

$$
\mathcal{L}=\frac12\left[-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{e^{\mathrm{logits}_{ii}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{\mathrm{logits}_{ij}}}\;-\;\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\frac{e^{\mathrm{logits}_{jj}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\mathrm{logits}_{ij}}}\right]
$$

**Where:**
- $N$: the batch size (32,768); $\mathrm{logits}_{ii}$: the score of the real pair $i$ (the diagonal).
- The first sum: for each image, a softmax over all $N$ texts, the loss being $-\log$ of the probability of its own text (cross-entropy over the row).
- The second sum: for each text, a softmax over all $N$ images, with the same loss for its own image (over the column).
- The $\frac12$: the average of the two directions, which makes the loss symmetric.

**Matters:** it pushes each real pair up and each of the $N^2-N$ wrong pairings down (1,073,709,056 wrong pairings per batch, my arithmetic). Using both directions teaches the model to retrieve text from an image and an image from text. Using many negatives per step is the point of the very large batch. Without the softmax (just maximising the diagonal) the model could make everything similar to everything.

**Example (tiny numbers):** a batch of three, with cosine similarities
$\begin{pmatrix}0.30&0.10&0.05\\0.08&0.25&0.12\\0.02&0.15&0.28\end{pmatrix}$.
- Scale 1: row losses $0.955,\ 1.001,\ 0.974$; column losses $0.939,\ 1.017,\ 0.973$; total $0.977$ (guessing gives $\ln3=1.099$).
- Scale $14.3$: row losses $0.082,\ 0.219,\ 0.166$; column losses $0.060,\ 0.305,\ 0.130$; total $0.160$.
- Scale 100: about $0$.
- Meaning: with a good scale the diagonal wins clearly, and the loss falls; the scale is what lets a small cosine gap produce a confident answer.

**3. The zero-shot classifier** (paper, 3.1.2)

$$
P(y=k\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{\exp\!\big(\cos(f(\mathbf{x}),g(c_k))/\tau\big)}{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\exp\!\big(\cos(f(\mathbf{x}),g(c_m))/\tau\big)}
$$

**Where:**
- $\mathbf{x}$: the image; $f(\mathbf{x})$: its embedding.
- $c_k$: the sentence written for class $k$ (such as "A photo of a dog."); $g(c_k)$: its embedding.
- $K$: the number of classes; $\cos$: cosine similarity; $\tau$: the temperature (so $1/\tau=e^{t}$).
- $P(y=k\mid\mathbf{x})$: the chance the image is in class $k$.

**Matters:** this is a logistic regression whose weights are the normalised text embeddings, with no bias term (paper, 3.1.2): the text encoder writes the classifier, the image encoder is the feature extractor. Adding a new class means adding a sentence. Without the temperature the probabilities would be nearly equal (example under equation 1).

**Example (tiny numbers):** the image and classes "plane", "car", "dog" give cosines $0.20,\ 0.24,\ 0.31$.
- With $1/\tau=100$: weights $e^{20},e^{24},e^{31}$ and probabilities $0.000,\ 0.001,\ 0.999$.
- The prediction is "dog".
- Meaning: the class whose sentence is closest to the picture wins, with a probability set by the scale.

**4. Prompt ensembling in embedding space** (paper, 3.1.4, described in words; the formula is my own notation)

$$
\bar{g}(c)=\frac{\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}g(p_m(c))}{\left\lVert\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}g(p_m(c))\right\rVert}
$$

**Where:**
- $c$: the class name; $p_m(c)$: the class name placed in the $m$-th prompt template (for example "A photo of a big {label}." or "A photo of a small {label}.").
- $g(\cdot)$: the text embedding, of length 1; $M$: the number of templates (80 for ImageNet).
- The average is rescaled to length 1 again, since an average of unit vectors is shorter (my addition; the paper only says "averaged text embeddings").

**Matters:** the paper builds the ensemble "over the embedding space instead of probability space", so the averaged class embeddings are computed and cached once, and "the compute cost of the ensemble is the same as using a single classifier when amortized over many predictions" (paper, 3.1.4). It adds 3.5 points on ImageNet over the single default prompt.

**Example (tiny numbers):** two prompt embeddings $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ for one class, and an image embedding $(0.8,0.6)$.
- Against each prompt alone the cosines are $0.8$ and $0.6$.
- The average $(0.5,0.5)$ has length $0.707$; rescaled it is $(0.707,0.707)$, whose cosine with the image is $0.990$.
- Meaning: the averaged class vector sits between the two wordings and matches the image better than either alone.

## What they found
Each number is marked "abstract" (read on the arXiv abstract) or "paper" (read in the paper's body text, as extracted from the PDF).

### Why a contrastive goal (paper's Figure 2)
A 63-million-parameter transformer that predicts caption words learned to recognise ImageNet classes **3 times slower** than a simpler bag-of-words prediction baseline, and swapping to the contrastive goal gave **another 4 times** efficiency gain (paper, 2.3 and Figure 2, read from the text). The figure itself is a plot I did not look at.

### Zero-shot against earlier zero-shot work (paper's Table 1)

|---|---|---|---|
| Visual N-Grams | 72.4 | 11.5 | 23.0 |
| CLIP | 98.4 | 76.2 | 58.5 |
| Gain, in points (mine) | 26.0 | 64.7 | 35.5 |

What each column is, and why it matters:
- **aYahoo, ImageNet, SUN:** three image classification data sets: aYahoo (everyday object categories), ImageNet (1,000 object classes) and SUN (scene categories) (general knowledge). The score is the percentage of images classified correctly with no training example from that data set (zero-shot).
- **Visual N-Grams:** the only earlier work that studied zero-shot transfer to ordinary classification data sets, so the best reference point (paper, 3.1.1).
- **Gain (mine):** CLIP minus Visual N-Grams.

**Summary: what the table shows.**
- **Biggest gain:** ImageNet, from 11.5 to 76.2, 6.6 times as high (mine), matching the original ResNet-50 without any of its 1.28 million labelled examples; top-5 was 95%, matching Inception-V4 (paper).
- **Smallest gain:** aYahoo, 26 points, since the old system already scored 72.4.
- **Caution:** the paper itself says this "should not be interpreted as a direct methods comparison": CLIP's data set is 10 times larger, its vision model needs nearly 100 times more compute per prediction, and it likely used over 1000 times the training compute (paper, 3.1.3). A CLIP ResNet-50 trained on the same YFCC100M data as Visual N-Grams was also tried; I did not read how it compared.

### Zero-shot against a supervised baseline (paper's Figure 5, from the text)
- Over 30 data sets were tested (abstract). On the 27-dataset suite zero-shot CLIP beat a fully supervised, regularised logistic regression on ResNet-50 features on **16 of 27** data sets, including ImageNet (paper, Figure 5 caption and 3.1.5). On Stanford Cars and Food101 it was ahead by over 20 points; on Flowers102 and FGVC Aircraft it was behind by over 10; on Kinetics700 it was ahead by 14.5 and on UCF101 by 7.7. On STL10 it reached 99.3% with no training example (paper, 3.1.5).
- Zero-shot CLIP matches the average of a 4-shot linear classifier on the same features, and roughly the best 16-shot classifier of the other public models they tested. The estimated number of labelled examples per class that a linear classifier needs to match zero-shot has a median of 5.4 and a mean of 20.8 (paper).
- **Prompt wording:** "A photo of a {label}." alone gave +1.3% on ImageNet, and ensembling 80 prompts added another +3.5%, so almost 5% in all (paper; my sum is 4.8).
- The Figure 5 per-dataset bars came out of the PDF text in a jumbled order, so I quote only the values the paper's text states.

**Summary: what these results show.**
- **Best:** object and general classification, and actions (nouns and verbs described in text), where CLIP matches or beats a supervised ResNet-50 probe; the authors suspect this is "due to natural language providing wider supervision for visual concepts involving verbs".
- **Worst:** specialised or abstract tasks (the limits section lists counting, distance and satellite classes) and fine-grained sets such as flowers and aircraft.
- **Caution:** the suite of 27 data sets was "somewhat haphazardly assembled" and co-adapted with the model by the authors (paper, 6).

### Features and robustness (paper, 3.2 and 3.3)
- As a feature extractor (linear probe), the best CLIP model beats the best existing model by an average of 2.6% over the 12-dataset suite of Kornblith et al. and by 5% over the paper's 27-dataset suite (paper). CLIP vision transformers were about 3 times more compute-efficient than CLIP ResNets (paper). It beats the Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2 on 21 of 27 data sets (paper).
- Zero-shot CLIP shrinks the gap between ImageNet accuracy and accuracy under natural distribution shift by up to 75% (paper). Adapting CLIP to ImageNet with a linear classifier raised ImageNet accuracy by 9.2% to 85.4%, but the average accuracy under distribution shift slightly decreased (paper).
- A check for leaked test images found that only 5 of 35 data sets showed a statistically significant accuracy difference from detected overlap, and the largest estimated overall gain was 0.6% on Birdsnap (paper).

### Scaling and cost
- Average zero-shot error over 36 data sets follows a smooth log-log trend across a 44-times range of compute for the 5 ResNet models, though single data sets are much noisier (paper).
- Eight models were trained for 32 epochs with a batch of 32,768 (above). The model called "CLIP" in the paper is ViT-L/14 trained for one more epoch at 336 pixels (paper, 2.5).

## Questions I had, answered

**Why predict which text goes with which image, and not the caption itself?** Predicting exact words is hard because the same picture has many valid captions. The paper tried it and found it learned ImageNet 3 times slower than a bag-of-words baseline; the contrastive goal then added a further 4 times (paper, 2.3). Deciding "which of these captions fits" is an easier question.

**Why does the temperature matter so much?** The cosines lie between $-1$ and $1$, and a softmax of numbers that close is nearly flat. In my example with cosines $0.31,0.24,0.20$, a scale of 1 gives $0.354,0.330,0.317$; a scale of 100 gives $0.999$ for the right one. The paper clips the scale at 100 to avoid unstable training (paper, 2.5).

**Why a huge batch?** In the loss every other pair in the batch is a negative, so a batch of 32,768 gives about 1.07 billion wrong pairings per step (my arithmetic). The paper does not test other batch sizes in the text I read.

**Isn't "zero-shot" cheating, since 400 million web pictures probably include ImageNet-like ones?** The paper checks: it measured overlap of test images with the training data and found only 5 of 35 data sets with a statistically significant difference, and at most 0.6% overall gain (Birdsnap) (paper, 5). They also say that the zero-shot setting means no examples from the *dataset* were used, not that the pre-training data had nothing similar.

**Why did a linear classifier with a few examples do worse than zero-shot?** The paper lists it as an oddity: fitting a linear classifier on one to a few examples gives a drop relative to zero-shot, "unlike humans", who gain a lot from one example (paper, limitations). I did not read an explanation in the parts I covered, so I give none.

## Why it mattered
It showed that the recipe that worked for language, namely web-scale pre-training with natural language and then prompting, also works for vision: one model could be pointed at a new classification task by writing the class names. The paper's conclusion says CLIP models learn many tasks during pre-training and that natural-language prompting turns this into zero-shot transfer (paper). The paper also released code and model weights (abstract). From memory (not checked here): CLIP-style image-text embeddings were later widely reused for image search and as parts of image generators and multimodal assistants.

## Limits and open questions
All from the paper's limitations section unless marked.
- **Not state of the art on its own:** on datasets with training sets, zero-shot CLIP is only competitive with a linear classifier on ResNet-50 features, and the authors estimate that about **1000 times more compute** would be needed to reach the overall state of the art, which they call infeasible with current hardware (paper).
- **Weak on some tasks:** fine-grained classes (car models, flower species, aircraft variants), counting objects, and novel tasks such as judging the distance to the nearest car, where it can be near random (paper). Zero-shot is 10 to 25 points below a linear probe on most datasets (paper).
- **Brittle outside its data:** it reaches only 88% on handwritten MNIST digits, and plain logistic regression on raw pixels does better, because almost nothing like MNIST is in its training data (paper).
- **Closed set:** it can only choose among the classes you write as text; it cannot generate a new answer the way a captioning model could (paper).
- **Data hunger:** the paper counts about 405 years to look at the 12.8 billion images seen over 32 epochs at one image per second (paper; my check: 405.6 years). The authors suggest combining it with self-supervision.
- **Evaluation caveats:** the authors queried full validation sets while developing the model, which is unrealistic for true zero-shot use, and their main 27-dataset suite was "somewhat haphazardly assembled" and co-adapted with the model (paper).
- **Biases:** the image-text pairs are unfiltered and uncurated, so the models learn social biases (paper). The paper has a broader-impacts section (section 7) that I did not read in detail.
- **Few-shot oddity:** fitting a linear classifier on a few examples gave a drop in performance relative to zero-shot, unlike humans, who gain a lot from one example (paper).
- **A small inconsistency in the paper:** the text says the byte-pair vocabulary has 49,152 words, while the common hyperparameter table (Table 18) says 49,408. Both give about 63 million parameters in the text encoder (my arithmetic: 37.7 million in the 12 blocks plus 25.2 to 25.3 million for the word table), so I cannot tell which is meant.

#flashcards/research

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP (Radford, Kim et al., OpenAI, 2021, arXiv 2103.00020): what problem did it tackle? (paper)::Image classifiers were trained for a fixed list of categories, so any new concept needed more labelled data. CLIP asked whether a model could learn directly from the text that sits next to pictures on the internet.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What did people do before CLIP?::Supervised pre-training on labelled sets such as ImageNet; weakly supervised pre-training on hashtags or noisy JFT classes (fixed 1000 or 18,291 classes with a fixed softmax); and small-scale learning from images with text (Visual N-Grams, VirTex, ICMLM, ConVIRT), which trained on only one to two hundred thousand images.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: what was the training task? (paper)::In a batch of N (image, text) pairs, predict which of the N × N possible pairings really occurred. Image and text encoders share an embedding space, with a symmetric cross-entropy loss over cosine similarities.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision ![[clip-contrastive-pretraining.png]]
What does this picture show?
?
CLIP's summary diagram (OpenAI's copy of the paper's Figure 1, MIT licence): (1) contrastive pre-training: a text encoder and an image encoder make N vectors each, and the N-by-N table of dot products should be high on the diagonal (real pairs); (2) class names go into "A photo of a {object}." and are encoded by the text encoder; (3) a new image is encoded and compared with them, and the best match is the zero-shot prediction.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: how big was the dataset and how was it built? (abstract, paper)::400 million (image, text) pairs from public internet sources. The authors searched for pairs whose text contained one of 500,000 queries and kept up to 20,000 pairs per query. Called WIT (WebImageText); its word count is similar to WebText.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What are CLIP's image and text encoders?::Image: a modified ResNet-50 with attention pooling in place of global average pooling, or a Vision Transformer (with an extra layer norm). Text: a 12-layer, 512-wide, 8-head masked Transformer with 63 million parameters, lower-cased byte-pair encoding, at most 76 tokens, with the activation at [EOS] as the text feature.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why does CLIP have a linear projection after each encoder, and no non-linear head?::The projection maps both kinds of feature into one shared embedding space of the same size so they can be compared. The authors saw no difference in training efficiency with a non-linear head, so they used the simpler linear one.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What simplifications does CLIP make compared with ConVIRT?::Trained from scratch (no ImageNet or pre-trained text weights); only a linear projection; a random square crop as the only augmentation; no sampling of a single sentence from the text; and a temperature that is learned rather than tuned. With 400 million pairs overfitting is not a major concern.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How is CLIP scaled (paper's Tables 19 and 20)?::Five ResNets (RN50, RN101 and RN50x4, x16, x64 with about 4, 16 and 64 times the compute of RN50, by growing width, depth and resolution together) and three ViTs (B/32, B/16, L/14, plus L/14 at 336 pixels). The text encoder stays at 12 layers; only its width grows. Embedding sizes run from 512 to 1024.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision What did CLIP's largest models cost to train?::RN50x64: 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs (about 29 GPU-years, my arithmetic); the largest ViT: 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs (about 8.4 GPU-years). 32 epochs of 400 million pairs is 12.8 billion images and 390,625 steps of 32,768 (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: what are the steps of one training step?::Take N pairs; encode images and texts; project and normalise to length 1; form the N-by-N table of scaled cosine similarities; treat each row and each column as a classification whose answer is the diagonal; average the two cross-entropy losses and update the encoders and temperature.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: how does zero-shot classification work? (paper)::Embed the class names as text (for example "A photo of a {label}."), embed the image, and pick the class with the highest cosine similarity. The paper views the text encoder as a hypernetwork that writes the weights of a linear classifier, which is computed once and cached.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Write CLIP's scaled similarity table and explain each symbol.
?
$$\mathrm{logits}_{ij}=e^{t}\,\frac{\mathbf{I}_i\cdot\mathbf{T}_j}{\lVert\mathbf{I}_i\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{T}_j\rVert}=e^{t}\,\mathbf{I}_{e,i}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{e,j}$$
- $\mathbf{I}_i,\mathbf{T}_j$: the projected image $i$ and text $j$ vectors, $\mathbf{I}_{e,i},\mathbf{T}_{e,j}$ the same scaled to length 1; the dot product is their cosine; $t$: the learned log-temperature, $e^t=1/\tau$, starting at $14.3$ and clipped at $100$.
- Normalising makes only direction matter; the scale sets how peaked the softmax is.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Scale example: cosines $0.31, 0.24, 0.20$ with the scale at 1, $14.3$ and 100. What are the probabilities?
?
Scale 1: $0.354, 0.330, 0.317$. Scale $14.3$: $0.635, 0.233, 0.132$. Scale 100: $0.999, 0.001, 0.000$ (my arithmetic). The same similarities become sure or unsure depending on the scale.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why does CLIP learn the temperature and clip it at 100?
?
Cosines lie in $[-1,1]$, so without a scale the softmax is nearly flat (probabilities $0.354, 0.330, 0.317$ in the example). Learning the scale avoids tuning it as a hyper-parameter, and clipping it at 100 (a $\tau$ of $0.01$) prevented unstable training, which the authors found necessary.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Write CLIP's symmetric contrastive loss and explain each symbol.
?
$$\mathcal{L}=\frac12\left[-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{e^{\mathrm{logits}_{ii}}}{\sum_{j}e^{\mathrm{logits}_{ij}}}\;-\;\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\frac{e^{\mathrm{logits}_{jj}}}{\sum_{i}e^{\mathrm{logits}_{ij}}}\right]$$
- $N$: batch size (32,768); $\mathrm{logits}_{ii}$: the real pair's score (diagonal); the first sum is a softmax over texts for each image (rows), the second a softmax over images for each text (columns); the $\frac12$ averages the two directions.
- It lifts each real pair and pushes down the $N^2-N$ wrong pairings.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Loss example: a batch of three with cosines $\begin{pmatrix}0.30&0.10&0.05\\0.08&0.25&0.12\\0.02&0.15&0.28\end{pmatrix}$. What is the loss at scale 1 and $14.3$?
?
Scale 1: total $0.977$ (rows $0.955, 1.001, 0.974$; columns $0.939, 1.017, 0.973$), barely below guessing, $\ln3=1.099$. Scale $14.3$: total $0.160$ (rows $0.082, 0.219, 0.166$). At scale 100 it is about $0$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why does the CLIP loss use both rows and columns?
?
Rows teach the model to pick the right text for an image and columns to pick the right image for a text; averaging them (the $\frac12$) makes the two embedding spaces equally good in both directions. With one direction only, the model could be good at one retrieval and poor at the other.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why does CLIP need a huge batch, and how many negatives does it get?
?
Every other pair in the batch is a negative: $N^2-N$ wrong pairings, which for $N=32{,}768$ is $1{,}073{,}709{,}056$ per step (my arithmetic), so each real pair is compared against about 32,767 wrong texts and images. The paper shards the similarity table over GPUs so each computes only its part.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Write CLIP's zero-shot classifier and explain each symbol.
?
$$P(y=k\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{\exp\!\big(\cos(f(\mathbf{x}),g(c_k))/\tau\big)}{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\exp\!\big(\cos(f(\mathbf{x}),g(c_m))/\tau\big)}$$
- $f(\mathbf{x})$: the image embedding; $g(c_k)$: the embedding of the sentence for class $k$; $K$: number of classes; $\tau$: temperature.
- A logistic regression with L2-normalised inputs and weights, no bias and a temperature, whose weights come from the text encoder (a hypernetwork).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Zero-shot example: cosines $0.20, 0.24, 0.31$ for plane, car and dog, with $1/\tau=100$. What are the probabilities?
?
About $0.000, 0.001, 0.999$ (my arithmetic): the picture is called "dog". The class whose sentence is closest wins, with a confidence set by the scale.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why does the zero-shot classifier have no bias term and normalised weights?
?
Because the weights are the normalised text embeddings, a score is just a cosine between two unit vectors, so classes are compared only by direction and none gets an unfair head start. A new class costs one sentence and no training data. Without normalisation a class with a longer vector would win more often.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Write the embedding-space prompt ensemble and explain each symbol.
?
$$\bar{g}(c)=\frac{\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}g(p_m(c))}{\left\lVert\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}g(p_m(c))\right\rVert}$$
- $p_m(c)$: the class name in the $m$-th template; $g$: the unit-length text embedding; $M$: the number of templates (80 for ImageNet).
- Averaging in embedding space gives one cached vector per class, so the ensemble costs no more than one prompt; the paper only says "averaged text embeddings" (the formula is my notation).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Ensemble example: prompt embeddings $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, image $(0.8,0.6)$. What are the cosines?
?
Each prompt alone: $0.8$ and $0.6$. The average $(0.5,0.5)$ has length $0.707$; rescaled to $(0.707,0.707)$ its cosine with the image is $0.990$ (my arithmetic). It matches better than either wording alone.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision Why average prompt embeddings and not probabilities?
?
Averaging embeddings gives one vector per class that can be computed once and cached, so the ensemble costs the same as a single prompt over many predictions; averaging probabilities would need a forward pass of the classifier per template. On ImageNet 80 templates added 3.5 points (paper).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: why did the authors choose a contrastive goal over predicting the caption? (paper)::A 63-million-parameter transformer predicting caption words learned ImageNet classes 3 times slower than a bag-of-words baseline, and the contrastive goal gave a further 4 times efficiency gain (Figure 2 and its text).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: what was the headline ImageNet result? (abstract, paper)::76.2% zero-shot top-1, matching the original ResNet-50 without using any of its 1.28 million training examples. Top-5 was 95%. The earlier Visual N-Grams had 11.5%.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How did CLIP compare with Visual N-Grams (paper's Table 1), and why is it not a fair test?::aYahoo 98.4 against 72.4, ImageNet 76.2 against 11.5 and SUN 58.5 against 23.0 (gains of 26.0, 64.7 and 35.5 points, my arithmetic). The paper says it is not a controlled comparison: 10 times more data, nearly 100 times more compute per prediction and likely over 1000 times the training compute.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision How did zero-shot CLIP compare with a supervised ResNet-50 linear probe? (paper)::It won on 16 of 27 data sets, including ImageNet: over 20 points ahead on Stanford Cars and Food101, 14.5 ahead on Kinetics700, 7.7 on UCF101; over 10 behind on Flowers102 and FGVC Aircraft. On STL10 it reached 99.3%. The authors suspect language gives wider supervision for verbs.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: how much did prompt wording matter on ImageNet? (paper)::"A photo of a {label}." alone gave +1.3%. Ensembling 80 prompts gave another +3.5%. About 5% in total (4.8 by my sum). Category hints such as "a type of pet" helped on fine-grained sets.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: how did zero-shot compare with few-shot linear classifiers? (paper)::Zero-shot CLIP matched the average of a 4-shot linear classifier on the same features, and roughly the best 16-shot classifier of the other public models tested. Median examples per class needed to match: 5.4, mean 20.8.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: what did it find about robustness? (paper)::Zero-shot CLIP shrinks the gap between ImageNet accuracy and accuracy under natural distribution shift by up to 75%. Adapting to ImageNet raised ImageNet accuracy by 9.2% to 85.4% but slightly lowered the average accuracy under shift.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: what training setup did the paper report? (paper)::8 models (5 ResNets, 3 Vision Transformers), 32 epochs, batch size 32,768, weight decay 0.2, 2,000 warm-up iterations. Temperature starts at the equivalent of 0.07, clipped so logits scale by at most 100.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-vision CLIP: what are its main limits? (paper)::About 1000 times more compute would be needed for zero-shot to reach the overall state of the art. Weak on fine-grained classes, counting and novel tasks (near random on distance to the nearest car). Only 88% on handwritten MNIST. It can only choose among the classes you write. It learns biases from uncurated pairs.
