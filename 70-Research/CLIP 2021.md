# CLIP 2021

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** Show a computer 400 million pictures that each come with a caption, and teach it to match each picture to its own caption. Then you can ask it "which of these sentences fits this new picture?", and it can answer about things nobody ever labelled for it.

Paper: "Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision", arXiv 2103.00020, first posted 26 February 2021 (paper). Concept note: [[CLIP]].

## The problem
Image classifiers of the time were trained to pick from a fixed list of categories (for example the 1000 ImageNet classes). The abstract says that this limits their generality, because more labelled data is needed to specify any other visual concept. Could a model learn visual ideas directly from the huge amount of text that already sits next to pictures on the internet, as language models had done for text? (abstract, paper)

## What people did before
- **Supervised pre-training on labelled sets:** train on [[Image Classification]] data such as ImageNet, then reuse the model. The paper notes that this was still standard practice in vision (paper).
- **Weakly supervised pre-training:** predicting hashtags on Instagram images (Mahajan et al., 2018) or the noisy classes of the JFT-300M dataset (used by BiT and [[ViT 2020]]). The paper says both designed and limited their supervision to 1000 and 18,291 classes, and used a fixed [[Softmax]] classifier, which limits zero-shot use (paper).
- **Learning from text paired with images:** the paper lists earlier work, from 1999 on. Visual N-Grams (Li et al., 2017) reached 11.5% zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet, which the paper calls well below the 88.4% state of the art of the time (paper). VirTex, ICMLM and ConVIRT showed the potential of transformers, masked language modelling and contrastive goals, but trained for "accelerator days" on one to two hundred thousand images (paper).
- **Predicting the caption:** the authors' own first attempt, a CNN plus a text [[Transformer]] trained to predict each caption word by word, learned too slowly (see "What they found").

## The idea
1. **A big dataset of pairs.** The authors built a new set of 400 million (image, text) pairs from public internet sources, called WIT (WebImageText). They searched for pairs whose text contained one of 500,000 queries and kept up to 20,000 pairs per query to balance the classes (paper).
2. **A simpler goal than predicting words.** Instead of writing the exact caption, the model only has to say **which text goes with which image** in a batch. In a batch of $N$ pairs it must pick the right one out of $N\times N$ possible pairings (paper). This is [[Contrastive Learning]]. The paper credits the batch construction to the N-pair loss (Sohn, 2016), popularised as InfoNCE by Oord et al. (2018), and adapted to (text, image) pairs for medical images by ConVIRT (Zhang et al., 2020) (paper).
3. **Two encoders, one shared space.** An image encoder and a text encoder map into one [[Embedding]] space. Training makes the [[Cosine Similarity]] high for the $N$ real pairs and low for the $N^2-N$ wrong ones, with a symmetric [[Cross-Entropy Loss]] (paper). The paper gives NumPy-style pseudocode (its Figure 3).
4. **Zero-shot use.** To classify an image, embed the names of the classes as text (for example "A photo of a {label}."), and pick the class whose text embedding is closest to the image embedding. The paper views the text encoder as a "hypernetwork" that writes the weights of a linear classifier (paper). Choosing and ensembling prompt wording is a form of [[Prompt Engineering]].

Simplifications the paper reports, compared with ConVIRT (paper): trained from scratch without ImageNet or pre-trained text weights; only a linear projection into the shared space (no non-linear projection); a random square crop as the only image augmentation; and a temperature $\tau$ that is learned rather than tuned.

## What they found
Each number is marked "abstract" (read on the arXiv abstract) or "paper" (read in the paper's body text, as extracted from the PDF).

**The data and training**
- 400 million (image, text) pairs (abstract and paper); the dataset has a similar total word count to WebText, the data used to train GPT-2 (paper).
- Eight models were trained: 5 ResNets and 3 Vision Transformers, for 32 epochs, with a batch size of 32,768 (paper). The largest ResNet (RN50x64) took 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs and the largest ViT took 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs (paper). The model called "CLIP" in the paper is ViT-L/14 trained for one more epoch at 336 pixels (paper).
- The text encoder is a 12-layer, 512-wide [[Transformer]] with 63 million parameters, using [[Byte-Pair Encoding]] with a 49,152-word vocabulary and a maximum length of 76 tokens (paper).
- The learned temperature starts at the equivalent of 0.07 and is clipped so that it never scales the logits by more than 100 (paper). Training used [[Adam Optimizer]] (decoupled weight decay), [[Mixed Precision Training]] and [[Gradient Checkpointing]] (paper).

**Why a contrastive goal**
- A 63-million-parameter transformer that predicts caption words learned to recognise ImageNet classes **3 times slower** than a simpler bag-of-words prediction baseline, and swapping to the contrastive goal gave **another 4 times** efficiency gain (paper, Figure 2).

**Zero-shot results**
- ImageNet zero-shot: **76.2%** top-1, matching the original ResNet-50 without using any of its 1.28 million training examples (abstract and paper). Top-5 was 95%, matching Inception-V4 (paper). Visual N-Grams had 11.5% (paper).
- On two other datasets in the paper's Table 1: aYahoo 98.4% against 72.4%, and SUN 58.5% against 23.0% (paper). The paper warns that this is not a controlled method comparison (the dataset is 10 times larger and the compute much higher).
- Over 30 datasets were tested (abstract). Zero-shot CLIP beat a fully supervised linear classifier on ResNet-50 features on **16 of 27 datasets** (paper). On STL10 it reached 99.3% with no training example (paper).
- Prompt wording: "A photo of a {label}." alone gave +1.3% on ImageNet, and ensembling 80 prompts added another +3.5%, so almost 5% in all (paper).
- Zero-shot CLIP matches the average of a 4-shot linear classifier on the same features, and roughly the best 16-shot classifier of the other public models they tested. The estimated number of labelled examples per class a linear classifier needs to match zero-shot has a median of 5.4 and a mean of 20.8 (paper).

**Features and robustness**
- As a feature extractor (linear probe), the best CLIP model beats the best existing model by an average of 2.6% over the 12-dataset suite of Kornblith et al. and by 5% over the paper's 27-dataset suite (paper). CLIP vision transformers were about 3 times more compute-efficient than CLIP ResNets (paper). It beats the Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2 on 21 of 27 datasets (paper).
- Zero-shot CLIP shrinks the gap between ImageNet accuracy and accuracy under natural distribution shift by up to 75% (paper). Adapting CLIP to ImageNet with a linear classifier raised ImageNet accuracy by 9.2% to 85.4%, but the average accuracy under distribution shift slightly decreased (paper).
- A check for leaked test images found that only 5 of 35 datasets showed a statistically significant accuracy difference from detected overlap, and the largest estimated overall gain was 0.6% on Birdsnap (paper).

**Scaling**
- Average zero-shot error over 36 datasets follows a smooth log-log trend across a 44-times range of compute for the 5 ResNet models, though single datasets are much noisier (paper).

## Why it mattered
It showed that the recipe that worked for language, namely web-scale pre-training with natural language and then prompting, also works for vision: one model could be pointed at a new classification task by writing the class names. The paper's conclusion says CLIP models learn many tasks during pre-training and that natural-language prompting turns this into zero-shot transfer (paper). The paper also released code and model weights (abstract). From memory (not checked here): CLIP-style image-text embeddings were widely reused afterwards, for example in image search and in text-guided image generation.

## Limits and open questions
All from the paper's limitations section unless marked.
- **Not state of the art on its own:** on datasets with training sets, zero-shot CLIP is only competitive with a linear classifier on ResNet-50 features, and the authors estimate that about **1000 times more compute** would be needed to reach the overall state of the art, which they call infeasible with current hardware (paper).
- **Weak on some tasks:** fine-grained classes (car models, flower species, aircraft variants), counting objects, and novel tasks such as judging the distance to the nearest car, where it can be near random (paper). Zero-shot is 10 to 25 points below a linear probe on most datasets (paper).
- **Brittle outside its data:** it reaches only 88% on handwritten MNIST digits, and plain logistic regression on raw pixels does better, because almost nothing like MNIST is in its training data (paper).
- **Closed set:** it can only choose among the classes you write as text; it cannot generate a new answer the way a captioning model could (paper).
- **Data hunger:** the paper counts about 405 years to look at the 12.8 billion images seen over 32 epochs at one image per second (paper). The authors suggest combining it with self-supervision.
- **Evaluation caveats:** the authors queried full validation sets while developing the model, which is unrealistic for true zero-shot use, and their main 27-dataset suite was "somewhat haphazardly assembled" and co-adapted with the model (paper).
- **Biases:** the image-text pairs are unfiltered and uncurated, so the models learn social biases (paper). The paper has a broader-impacts section (section 7) that I did not read in detail.
- **Few-shot oddity:** fitting a linear classifier on a few examples gave a drop in performance relative to zero-shot, unlike humans, who gain a lot from one example (paper).
