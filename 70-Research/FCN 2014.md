# FCN 2014

> **In one line for a 12-year-old:** A picture-recognising network normally says one thing about the whole picture ("cat"). Cut off its last dense layers and turn them into sliding filters, and it instead gives an answer for every spot of the picture, and a few shortcuts then sharpen the edges.

Paper: "Fully Convolutional Networks for Semantic Segmentation", arXiv 1411.4038, the version read is v2 of 8 March 2015, 9 pages plus the appendices (the paper was first posted in November 2014, from the arXiv number). Marks: "(paper, section)" was read in the paper; "(general knowledge)" is my own background; "(mine)" is my own arithmetic, done in Python. This paper's arXiv licence is not open (the usual arXiv licence), so its own figures are not copied; the note uses one free CC0 picture of a segmentation mask, which is not the paper's.

## The problem
Semantic segmentation means giving every pixel the class of the object or region it belongs to (see [[Image Segmentation]]). The paper says earlier convnet approaches had "shortcomings that this work addresses": they were trained on small patches, used small networks without supervised pre-training, and needed extra machinery such as superpixels, proposals, random-field clean-up or ensembles (paper, 1 and 2). The other tension is built into the task: "global information resolves what while local information resolves where" (paper, 1). A deep layer knows what an object is but sees it coarsely; a shallow layer knows exactly where the edges are but not what they mean.

## What people did before
- **Patchwise training:** classify a small patch around each pixel (Ciresan et al., Farabet et al., Pinheiro and Collobert and others); "common, but lacks the efficiency of fully convolutional training" (paper, 1 and 2).
- **Hybrid proposal-classifier models** (Hariharan et al.'s SDS, Gupta et al.): fine-tune an R-CNN system that samples boxes or region proposals, so they were "not learned end-to-end" (paper, 2). SDS was the previous state of the art on PASCAL VOC, so it is the paper's main comparison (paper, 2 and 5). See [[R-CNN 2013]].
- **Convolutional computation on any size of input:** Matan et al. (LeNet for strings of digits), Wolf and Platt, Ning et al. and OverFeat (Sermanet et al.) already used fully convolutional inference, but "fully convolutional training is rare" (paper, 2).
- **Fixed-input classifiers:** AlexNet, [[VGG 2014]] and [[GoogLeNet 2014]] take fixed-size inputs and give one label; their fully connected layers "throw away spatial coordinates" (paper, 3.1). See [[LeNet and AlexNet]].

## The idea
Three observations make a classifier into a segmenter (paper, 3 and 4):
1. **A fully connected layer is a convolution** whose kernel covers its whole input. Convert them all, and the network accepts an image of any size and outputs a map of class scores (a heatmap) instead of one vector.
2. **Upsample inside the network.** The map is smaller than the image (stride 32). A learnable "backwards strided convolution" (see [[Transposed Convolution]]) brings it back to pixel size, so the loss can be computed on every pixel and the whole net trained end to end.
3. **Combine coarse and fine.** Add "skip" links that sum the coarse prediction (semantic, stride 32) with predictions from shallower layers (appearance, strides 16 and 8) to sharpen the result.

Fine-tune from the ImageNet classification network, so the segmentation net starts with good features (paper, 4). See [[Transfer Learning]] and [[Fine-tuning]].

The whole design (paper, 3 and 4). Take a classification network (AlexNet, VGG-16 or GoogLeNet), "decapitate" it by discarding the final classifier layer, and convert its fully connected layers to convolutions. Add a 1 by 1 convolution with 21 channels (the 20 PASCAL classes plus background) to score every class at each coarse output position, then a deconvolution layer that bilinearly upsamples the scores to the image size (paper, 4.1). The best model, FCN-8s, also fuses the predictions of pool4 and pool3 (paper, 4.2).

### Part by part
- **Convolutionalisation of the fully connected layers.**
  - *What it does:* a fully connected layer is viewed as a convolution whose kernel is as big as its input, so VGG-16's fc6 becomes a 7 by 7 convolution with 4096 filters and fc7 a 1 by 1 convolution (paper, 3.1).
  - *Why it matters:* the net now takes any image size and outputs a score map. The result is the same as running the original net on each patch, but the computation is shared over the overlapping parts: AlexNet takes 1.2 ms for a 227 by 227 image, and the fully convolutional version 22 ms for a 10 by 10 grid of outputs from a 500 by 500 image, which is "more than 5 times faster" than 100 separate patches (100 times 1.2 is 120 ms, so 5.5 times, mine) (paper, 3.1). The backward pass similarly takes 37 ms against 240 ms for 100 patches, 6.5 times (mine, from the paper's 2.4 ms per image).
- **The 21-channel score layer.**
  - *What it does:* a 1 by 1 convolution gives 21 class scores at every coarse position, zero-initialised ("random initialization yields neither better performance nor faster convergence", paper, 4.3).
  - *Why it matters:* it is the only new layer in FCN-32s; everything else is inherited from the classifier.
- **In-network upsampling by backwards strided convolution (deconvolution).**
  - *What it does:* upsampling by a factor $f$ is "convolution with a fractional input stride of $1/f$", so a layer with output stride $f$ does it, "simply reversing the forward and backward passes of convolution". It starts as bilinear interpolation and can be learned (paper, 3.3).
  - *Why it matters:* it lets the pixelwise loss train the upsampling by backpropagation, instead of fixing it by hand. The final layer's filters are kept fixed at bilinear; the intermediate ones start bilinear and are learned (paper, 4.3).
- **Skip links: FCN-32s, FCN-16s and FCN-8s.**
  - *What they do:* FCN-32s upsamples the stride-32 prediction in one step. FCN-16s adds a 1 by 1 prediction on pool4 (stride 16), fuses it with a 2 times upsampled stride-32 prediction by *summing*, and upsamples 16 times. FCN-8s then fuses pool3 (stride 8) the same way and upsamples 8 times (paper, 4.2; sizes mine: 2 times 16 is 32, and 2 times 2 times 8 is also 32).
  - *Why they matter:* "combining fine layers and coarse layers lets the model make local predictions that respect global structure"; the paper calls the feature hierarchy the "deep jet". The new pool4 and pool3 layers are zero-initialised, so the net starts with the old predictions, and the learning rate is divided by 100 for this stage (paper, 4.2).
- **Why summing and not taking the maximum.** "Max fusion made learning difficult due to gradient switching" (paper, footnote 6).
- **Whole-image training instead of patches.**
  - *What it does:* the loss is the sum over all output positions, so training on a whole image equals training on a batch of all its receptive fields (equation 3).
  - *Why it matters:* it is much more efficient than patch sampling and no worse: sampling a fraction of the positions (50%, 25%, 10%) did not speed convergence per iteration and was slower in wall time (paper, 4.3 and Figure 5). Class balancing was "unnecessary" though about 3/4 of the labels are background (paper, 4.3).
- **Alternatives the paper tried and rejected.**
  - *Shift-and-stitch* (OverFeat's trick: run the net on $f^2$ shifted copies and interleave the outputs) is equivalent to enlarging the filters by inserting zeros ("filter rarefaction"). The paper found its cost to improvement ratio "worse than layer fusion" and did not use it (paper, 3.2 and 4.2).
  - *Making pool5 stride 1* would need a 14 by 14 fc6 filter and the authors could not learn it well (paper, 4.2).

## The core mechanism, step by step

### Turning a classifier into a segmentation network (my numbered paraphrase of paper, 3 and 4)
1. **Start from a trained classifier** (VGG-16).
2. **Convert its fully connected layers into convolutions:** fc6 to a 7 by 7 convolution with 4096 filters, fc7 to a 1 by 1 convolution with 4096 filters.
3. **Discard the 1000-class layer** and add a zero-initialised 1 by 1 convolution with 21 outputs.
4. **Upsample** the coarse score map back to the image size with a bilinear-initialised deconvolution layer.
5. **Train all layers** with a per-pixel multinomial logistic loss (ignoring pixels masked as ambiguous), on whole images.
6. **Refine with skips:** first FCN-32s, then add pool4 to get FCN-16s, then pool3 to get FCN-8s; each is initialised from the previous one.

**Worked example (tiny numbers, mine).** For a 500 by 500 image the stride-32 map has about 15 by 15 positions; FCN-16s adds a 31 by 31 map from pool4, and FCN-8s a 62 by 62 map from pool3 (rounded down, since the paper's padding scheme is not given in the text I read). For a 224 by 224 image the maps are 7 by 7, 14 by 14 and 28 by 28. A 2 by 2 toy of the fusion: coarse scores $\begin{pmatrix}2.0&-1.0\\0.5&3.0\end{pmatrix}$ upsampled by 2 give a 4 by 4 map (each score repeated in a 2 by 2 block, nearest neighbour for the picture; the paper uses bilinear), and adding the pool4 scores for the first row $[0.1,0.0,-0.2,0.0]$ gives $[2.1,\ 2.0,\ -1.2,\ -1.0]$. The fine map nudges the coarse one by small amounts.

### How good can a coarse prediction be? (paper's Appendix A)

The paper computes how good a prediction could be at best for a given output stride, by downsampling the ground truth and upsampling it again.

**Table: upper bounds on mean IU for a given downsampling factor** (written by me from the paper's appendix table).

| Downsampling factor | 128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Best possible mean IU (%) | 50.9 | 73.3 | 86.1 | 92.8 | 96.4 | 98.5 |

What the columns mean, and why it matters: the factor is the stride of the prediction; the bound is the mean IU of a perfect prediction made at that stride. Even at stride 32 a perfect coarse prediction reaches 86.1, so the net's 56 to 62 is far from the ceiling of its own resolution, and "pixel-perfect prediction is clearly not necessary to achieve mean IU well above state-of-the-art". The paper concludes that mean IU "is not a good measure of fine-scale accuracy" (paper, Appendix A).

## The equations

**1. The layer rule of a deep filter** (paper, section 3, unnumbered)

$$
y_{ij}=f_{ks}\big(\{x_{si+\delta i,\;sj+\delta j}\}_{0\le\delta i,\delta j\le k}\big)
$$

**Where:**
- $x_{ij}$: the data vector at position $(i,j)$ in a layer; $y_{ij}$: the same in the next layer.
- $k$: the kernel size (the window is $k\times k$); $s$: the stride (the subsampling factor).
- $f_{ks}$: what the layer does with its window: a matrix multiplication for convolution or average pooling, a spatial maximum for max pooling, an element-wise function for an activation.
- $\delta i,\delta j$: the offsets inside the window.

**Matters:** every layer of a convolutional network (convolution, pooling, activation) has this form, depending only on relative positions. So a network built only from such layers "computes a nonlinear filter", a deep filter, which works on any input size and gives an output of corresponding size. A single fully connected layer breaks the form, since it needs a fixed input size.

**Example (tiny numbers):** one dimension, a maximum with $k=2$ and $s=2$ over $x=(3,1,4,1,5,9)$.
- $y_0=\max(x_0,x_1)=\max(3,1)=3$.
- $y_1=\max(x_2,x_3)=\max(4,1)=4$; $y_2=\max(x_4,x_5)=\max(5,9)=9$.
- Meaning: 6 inputs become 3 outputs, each reading a window two wide, moving 2 at a time. A longer input would just give more outputs.

**2. The composition rule of kernel size and stride** (paper, section 3, unnumbered)

$$
f_{ks}\circ g_{k's'}=(f\circ g)_{\,k'+(k-1)s',\;ss'}
$$

**Where:**
- $g_{k's'}$: the earlier layer (kernel $k'$, stride $s'$); $f_{ks}$: the layer applied after it.
- $f\circ g$: the two layers taken as one; its kernel size is $k'+(k-1)s'$ (the area of the input one output sees) and its stride $ss'$.

**Matters:** the form is "maintained under composition", so a whole stack is again a deep filter. The formula gives a unit's receptive field and the stride of the output map without counting by hand, which is why the final map of a classifier is coarse by a factor equal to the product of all strides. Without it the stride-32 and the receptive field of 404 could not be predicted.

**Example (tiny numbers):** two 3 by 3 convolutions with stride 1 and then a 2 by 2 pooling with stride 2.
- First: $k'=3$, $s'=1$. Add a 3 by 3 convolution on top: $k=3+(3-1)\times1=5$, $s=1$.
- Add the pooling ($k=2$, $s=2$): $k=5+(2-1)\times1=6$, $s=1\times2=2$.
- For the whole of VGG-16 up to pool5 the same rule gives a window of 212 pixels and stride 32 (mine), and after the 7 by 7 fc6 a window of $212+6\times32=404$, as in the paper's Table 1.
- Meaning: after the first pooling each output sees a 6 by 6 patch and moves 2 pixels at a time.

**3. The loss as a sum over positions** (paper, section 3, unnumbered)

$$
\ell(\mathbf{x};\theta)=\sum_{ij}\ell'(\mathbf{x}_{ij};\theta)
$$

**Where:**
- $\mathbf{x}$: the input image; $\theta$: all the weights of the network.
- $\mathbf{x}_{ij}$: the data at one position of the final layer; $\ell'$: the loss at that one position; $\ell$: the loss of the whole image.

**Matters:** the gradient of a sum is the sum of the gradients, so stochastic gradient descent on the loss of whole images equals stochastic gradient descent on $\ell'$ "taking all of the final layer receptive fields as a minibatch". It is why whole-image training is the same as patchwise training with a particular batch, but much cheaper, since the overlapping patches share their computation. See [[Stochastic Gradient Descent]].

**Example (tiny numbers):** a toy image with 4 output positions and true-class probabilities of 0.9, 0.5, 0.8 and 0.3.
- Per-position losses $-\ln p$: $0.105$, $0.693$, $0.223$ and $1.204$.
- The image loss is the sum, $2.226$ (the mean, $0.556$, differs only by a constant).
- Meaning: the 4 positions behave like a batch of 4 independent samples, and the hardest one (0.3) contributes half of the loss.

**4. The four segmentation metrics** (paper, section 5)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pixel acc.}&=\frac{\sum_i n_{ii}}{\sum_i t_i},\qquad
\text{mean acc.}=\frac{1}{n_{\text{cl}}}\sum_i\frac{n_{ii}}{t_i},\\
\text{mean IU}&=\frac{1}{n_{\text{cl}}}\sum_i\frac{n_{ii}}{t_i+\sum_j n_{ji}-n_{ii}},\qquad
\text{f.w. IU}=\Big(\sum_k t_k\Big)^{-1}\sum_i\frac{t_i\,n_{ii}}{t_i+\sum_j n_{ji}-n_{ii}}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Where:**
- $n_{ij}$: the number of pixels of class $i$ predicted to be class $j$; $n_{ii}$: the correctly predicted ones.
- $t_i=\sum_j n_{ij}$: the total number of pixels whose true class is $i$.
- $n_{\text{cl}}$: the number of classes (including background).
- $t_i+\sum_j n_{ji}-n_{ii}$: the pixels of class $i$ plus the pixels predicted as $i$, minus the overlap counted twice, that is the union of "truly $i$" and "predicted $i$".

**Matters:** pixel accuracy is dominated by big classes (a net that says "background" everywhere scores high). Mean accuracy fixes that by averaging over classes. Mean IU also punishes false alarms, since its denominator contains the pixels wrongly predicted as $i$, so it is the strictest; the frequency-weighted IU weights classes by their size. The paper's test server provides only mean IU. The paper's acknowledgements thank a reader for pointing out an error in its frequency-weighted formula, so the version printed here is the corrected one.

**Example (tiny numbers):** three classes (background, cat, dog), rows the true class and columns the prediction, $n=\begin{pmatrix}50&10&0\\5&20&5\\0&5&5\end{pmatrix}$, so $t=(60,30,10)$ and 100 pixels in total.
- Pixel accuracy: $(50+20+5)/100=0.75$.
- Mean accuracy: $(50/60+20/30+5/10)/3=0.667$.
- IU per class: background $50/(60+55-50)=0.769$, cat $20/(30+35-20)=0.444$, dog $5/(10+10-5)=0.333$; mean IU $=0.516$.
- Frequency-weighted IU: $(60\times0.769+30\times0.444+10\times0.333)/100=0.628$.
- A net that says "background" for every pixel: pixel accuracy $0.60$ but mean IU only $0.20$ (background IU 0.6, the others 0).
- Meaning: 75% of pixels are right but the dog is found with an overlap of a third, which is what mean IU makes visible.

## What they found
The headline numbers (paper): FCN-8s reaches 62.2 mean IU on PASCAL VOC 2012 test, "20% relative improvement" over the previous best, 62.7 on VOC 2011 test, and state-of-the-art on NYUDv2 and SIFT Flow, while one image takes about 175 ms, "less than one fifth of a second" (abstract and Table 3). Inference is 114 times faster (convnet only) or 286 times (overall) than SDS (paper, 5).

### More results (paper's Appendix B)
On PASCAL-Context (59 most frequent classes), FCN-8s has 35.1 mean IU, "an 11% relative improvement" over the previous best, Convolutional Feature Masking (paper, Table 6); my back-calculation gives a previous value of about 31.6 (35.1 divided by 1.11). I did not read the rest of Table 6.

## Questions I had, answered

**Isn't a convolution with a 7 by 7 kernel over a 7 by 7 input the same as a fully connected layer?** Yes, when the input is exactly 7 by 7 there is one position, and the 7 by 7 by 512 inputs and the 4096 outputs are connected by the same $7\times7\times512\times4096=102.8$ M weights (mine). On a bigger image the filter slides, so the same weights produce a whole map of 4096-channel outputs (paper, 3.1).

**Why is the output of an FCN coarse?** Each pooling layer halves the map; VGG-16 has five, so the stride is $2^5=32$. One output position stands for a 32 by 32 block of pixels (paper, 3.1 and 4.2). The upsampling can only spread the coarse answer over those pixels; the skip links bring in finer information.

**What is "deconvolution" here?** Not the mathematical inverse of a convolution. It is a convolution run backwards: the forward and backward passes of a strided convolution swapped, so that the output is bigger than the input. The paper notes the name is only "sometimes called deconvolution" (paper, 3.3).

**Why does the paper say mean IU is not good for fine detail?** Because even a perfect answer at stride 32 scores 86.1 mean IU (Appendix A), so a large part of the score is reached without drawing edges exactly. The IU metric weights large regions, and thin structures and boundaries count for little.

**How can fine-tuning only the last layer give so little?** The ImageNet features were trained for whole-image classes, not for pixel labels; freezing all but the score layer gives 45.4 mean IU against 59.4 with all layers trained (paper's Table 2), so the lower layers must change as well.

## Why it mattered
It showed that a segmentation network can be a plain convolutional network trained pixels to pixels, with no proposals, patches or post-processing, and that classification networks can be reused for it by fine-tuning. The skip links that sum coarse and fine predictions, and the learned in-network upsampling, became standard parts of later segmentation designs, for example [[U-Net 2015]], which builds directly on it (U-Net says so itself). The idea of a fully convolutional head is also behind the region proposal network of [[Faster R-CNN 2015]], which the Faster R-CNN paper calls "a kind of fully convolutional network" (paper of Faster R-CNN, 1).

## Limits and open questions
- **Coarse output:** even with the skips, the finest stride is 8, and the paper says its fusion "met diminishing returns" there (4.2); the dense prediction is bilinearly upsampled, not decoded from fine features.
- **Mean IU does not measure edge accuracy** (Appendix A), so the fine detail the skips add is hard to see in the numbers: +0.3 mean IU from FCN-16s to FCN-8s.
- **Semantic, not instance, segmentation:** two objects of the same class touching each other get the same label; it cannot count objects. The paper does not address this.
- **Failure case:** in the paper's Figure 6 the net "sees lifejackets in a boat as people" (paper, Figure 6 caption).
- **Fixed settings:** the learning rates were chosen by line search for each network and are given as a fixed value, with training "sensitive to the learning rate" (paper, 4.3).
- **Training cost:** fine-tuning takes three days on one GPU for FCN-32s and about a day more for each of the 16s and 8s upgrades (paper, 4.3).
- **GoogLeNet underperforms** and the paper does not say why (4.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation FCN (Long, Shelhamer and Darrell, 2014, arXiv 1411.4038): what problem did it attack?::Semantic segmentation, a class for every pixel, which earlier convnet methods did with small patchwise-trained networks, no supervised pre-training and extra machinery (superpixels, proposals, random fields). The tension is that global information resolves what and local information resolves where (paper, 1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What did people do before FCN?::Patchwise training of small convnets, hybrid proposal-classifier models such as SDS (an R-CNN fine-tuned for segmentation, not end to end), and fully convolutional inference like OverFeat's, but fully convolutional training was rare (paper, 2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation FCN: what is the idea?::Make a classifier fully convolutional so it takes any size and gives a score map, upsample the map inside the network, fine-tune from ImageNet, train end to end on a pixelwise loss, and add skip links that sum coarse semantic and fine appearance predictions (paper, 1, 3, 4).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How do you turn a fully connected layer into a convolution?::View it as a convolution whose kernel covers its whole input: VGG-16's fc6 becomes a 7 by 7 convolution with 4096 filters and fc7 a 1 by 1 convolution. The net then accepts any image and outputs a heatmap of class scores (paper, 3.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why is the fully convolutional version of a classifier more efficient on a big image?::It equals running the net on each patch but shares the computation over the overlapping parts: AlexNet takes 22 ms for a 10 by 10 grid of outputs on a 500 by 500 image against 120 ms for 100 separate patches of 1.2 ms (5.5 times, my arithmetic); the backward pass is about 6.5 times faster (paper, 3.1).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What is the "deconvolution" layer in FCN?::Upsampling by a backwards strided convolution (output stride $f$ for a factor $f$): the forward and backward passes of a convolution swapped. It starts as bilinear interpolation and can be learned, so the pixelwise loss trains it; the final layer is fixed bilinear (paper, 3.3 and 4.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What are FCN-32s, FCN-16s and FCN-8s?::FCN-32s upsamples the stride-32 prediction in one step; FCN-16s sums it (2 times upsampled) with a prediction from pool4 and upsamples 16 times; FCN-8s adds pool3 the same way and upsamples 8 times. New layers are zero-initialised and the learning rate is divided by 100 (paper, 4.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why does FCN sum the skip predictions and not take the maximum?::"Max fusion made learning difficult due to gradient switching" (paper, footnote 6).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why is whole-image training enough, with no patch sampling or class balancing?::The loss is a sum over positions, so a whole image is a batch of all its receptive fields; sampling 50%, 25% or 10% of the positions did not speed convergence per iteration and was slower in wall time, and class balancing was unnecessary though about 3/4 of labels are background (paper, 4.3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation What alternatives did FCN try and drop?::Shift-and-stitch (OverFeat's trick; equal to filter rarefaction) because its cost to improvement ratio was worse than layer fusion, and stride-1 pool5 because it needs a 14 by 14 fc6 filter that they could not learn well (paper, 3.2 and 4.2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How do the three classifiers compare as FCNs (paper's Table 1: best, worst, caution)?::VGG-16 best at 56.0 mean IU (210 ms, 134 M parameters); AlexNet worst at 39.8 (50 ms, about 75.7% of SDS, my arithmetic); GoogLeNet 42.5 (59 ms, 6 M), 13.5 below VGG with no explanation. All have stride 32. Caution: best results at a fixed learning rate, and a validation score is compared with a test score.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation How big is FCN-VGG16 and what is its receptive field, by my count?::134.35 M weights (14.71 M convolutions, 102.76 M fc6, 16.78 M fc7, 86,037 for the 21-class layer), matching the paper's 134 M; the receptive field is 212 pixels at pool5 and 404 after fc6, with stride 32, matching the paper's 404 (my arithmetic with equation 2).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write the layer rule of a deep filter (paper, section 3) and explain each symbol.
?
$$y_{ij}=f_{ks}\big(\{x_{si+\delta i,\;sj+\delta j}\}_{0\le\delta i,\delta j\le k}\big)$$
- $x_{ij},y_{ij}$: data vectors at $(i,j)$ in a layer and in the next; $k$: kernel size; $s$: stride; $f_{ks}$: the layer type (matrix multiplication, maximum, element-wise function).
- Convolution, pooling and activation all have this form, so a stack of them is a filter that works on any input size.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Layer rule example: a maximum with $k=2$, $s=2$ over $(3,1,4,1,5,9)$. What is the output?
?
$\max(3,1)=3$, $\max(4,1)=4$, $\max(5,9)=9$, so $(3,4,9)$ (my arithmetic): 6 inputs become 3 outputs, each reading two numbers and moving 2 at a time.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why do the layers of a convolutional network have to follow this rule to be fully convolutional?
?
The rule depends only on relative positions, so the network works on any size and outputs a map of corresponding size. A fully connected layer breaks it because it needs a fixed input size: a 6 number row gave 3 outputs in the example, a longer row would just give more, but a dense layer of fixed width cannot do that.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write the composition rule for kernel size and stride (paper, section 3) and explain each symbol.
?
$$f_{ks}\circ g_{k's'}=(f\circ g)_{\,k'+(k-1)s',\;ss'}$$
- $g_{k's'}$: the earlier layer; $f_{ks}$: the later one; the pair behaves as one layer with kernel $k'+(k-1)s'$ and stride $ss'$.
- It gives the receptive field and the output stride of a whole stack.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Composition example: two 3 by 3 convolutions (stride 1) then a 2 by 2 pooling (stride 2). What are the kernel and stride?
?
$3\to3+2\times1=5$, then $5+(2-1)\times1=6$ with stride $1\times2=2$ (my arithmetic). VGG-16 up to pool5 gives 212 and stride 32; after the 7 by 7 fc6 it is $212+6\times32=404$, the paper's Table 1 value.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why is an FCN's output stride the product of all the strides, and what does it cost?
?
Each stride multiplies: five poolings of stride 2 give $2^5=32$, so one output position covers 32 by 32 pixels and a 500 by 500 image gives a map of about 15 by 15 (my arithmetic). The answer is coarse, which the skip links to pool4 (stride 16) and pool3 (stride 8) try to fix.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write FCN's loss as a sum over positions (paper, section 3) and explain each symbol.
?
$$\ell(\mathbf{x};\theta)=\sum_{ij}\ell'(\mathbf{x}_{ij};\theta)$$
- $\mathbf{x}$: the image; $\theta$: the weights; $\mathbf{x}_{ij}$: one position of the final layer; $\ell'$: the loss at that position.
- The gradient of the sum is the sum of the gradients, so SGD on whole images equals SGD on $\ell'$ with all the final-layer receptive fields as a minibatch.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Loss example: four positions with true-class probabilities 0.9, 0.5, 0.8 and 0.3. What is the image loss?
?
$-\ln$ of each: $0.105$, $0.693$, $0.223$, $1.204$; the sum is $2.226$ (my arithmetic), the mean $0.556$. The hardest position, 0.3, gives about half of the loss.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why is training on whole images the same as patchwise training, and why is it cheaper?
?
Because the loss is a sum over positions, its gradient is the sum of the per-position gradients, which is exactly a batch of all the receptive fields of the output units. It is cheaper because overlapping receptive fields share their computation layer by layer instead of recomputing each patch (paper, 3).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Write FCN's segmentation metrics (paper, section 5), at least mean IU, and explain each symbol.
?
$$\text{mean IU}=\frac{1}{n_{\text{cl}}}\sum_i\frac{n_{ii}}{t_i+\sum_j n_{ji}-n_{ii}}$$ (pixel accuracy is $\sum_i n_{ii}/\sum_i t_i$, mean accuracy $\frac1{n_{\text{cl}}}\sum_i n_{ii}/t_i$)
- $n_{ij}$: pixels of class $i$ predicted as $j$; $t_i=\sum_j n_{ij}$: pixels of true class $i$; $n_{\text{cl}}$: number of classes.
- The denominator is the union of "truly $i$" and "predicted $i$", so false alarms are punished.

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Metric example: confusion matrix rows (50,10,0), (5,20,5), (0,5,5) for background, cat and dog. What are the metrics?
?
Pixel accuracy $75/100=0.75$; mean accuracy $0.667$; IU $0.769$, $0.444$, $0.333$ so mean IU $0.516$; frequency-weighted IU $0.628$ (my arithmetic).

#flashcards/research/concept #flashcards/topic/research-detection-segmentation Why is mean IU stricter than pixel accuracy?
?
A net that says "background" for every pixel gets pixel accuracy $0.60$ in the example but mean IU $0.20$, because the cat and dog IU are 0 and background's IU is only 0.6. Pixel accuracy is dominated by big classes; mean IU averages over classes and counts false alarms (my arithmetic).
