# Reasoning Model

An [[LLM]] trained to **think before it answers**: it writes a long chain of reasoning first, and the final answer comes after.

## 🟢 1. Simple (age 12)
An ordinary chatbot starts answering straight away. A reasoning model first writes a long **scratchpad** where it tries ideas, checks its work and fixes mistakes, and only then gives you the answer. It was not taught *how* to think by humans writing out examples. It was **rewarded when its final answer turned out right**, and it worked out useful ways of thinking on its own.

**Example:** "A bear was born in 2020. How old is it now?" A plain model may blurt "4". A reasoning model writes: "It is 2025. 2025 − 2020 = 5. Let me check: 2020 + 5 = 2025. Yes." Then it answers "5".

## 🔵 2. How it works
1. Start from a normal model that already writes well ([[Pre-training]]).
2. Pick problems whose answers can be **checked by a program**: maths with a known result, code with test cases ([[Pass at k]]). This gives a reward without any human judge.
3. For each problem, let the model write a **group of attempts**, each in the form: thinking inside `<think>` tags, then the answer inside `<answer>` tags.
4. Give each attempt two kinds of reward: a **format** reward (are the tags there?) and an **accuracy** reward (is the answer correct?).
5. Use [[GRPO]] to make high-reward attempts more likely and low-reward attempts less likely.
6. Over many rounds, the model learns to use longer, more careful thinking, because careful thinking earns more reward. This is **scaling by reinforcement learning**, and the extra thinking at answer time is called **test-time compute**.
7. Costs: the answer takes longer and costs more tokens, because the thinking is written out (the lecture says providers price thinking tokens as output). The thinking is usually hidden or summarised to the user.
8. Smaller models can be taught the same style by **distillation**: train them on the long reasoning examples of a big one ([[Knowledge Distillation]]).

## 🟠 3. Technical detail
- **The idea in one line (lecture):** do chain of thought ([[Chain of Thought]]) at a much larger scale, so the output is *reasoning plus answer*.
- **Why not teach by examples:** the lecture gives three reasons: reasoning chains are hard to write by hand; you do not want to limit the model to human-style reasoning; and many problems have a natural verifiable reward (solved or not).
- **Rewards in the lecture's R1-Zero run:** (1) the thinking is wrapped in the tags, (2) the final answer is correct (maths: compare with the ground truth; code: run the test cases). In the later R1 run, a language-consistency term was added, and helpfulness and harmlessness for general prompts.
- **The DeepSeek-R1 recipe as the lecture presents it:** start from the V3-Base model (about 671B total and 37B active parameters, a mixture-of-experts); R1-Zero is GRPO on reasoning data with no supervised step and it gets reasoning abilities, but its chains have formatting and readability problems. R1 adds a small supervised step on long chains (generated by R1-Zero and rewritten by people), then GRPO again; then a large supervised step (about 200,000 general pairs and about 600,000 reasoning pairs picked by rejection sampling); then a final round of RL for reasoning and general helpfulness and harmlessness. The numbers are from the slides; I did not check them in the paper.
- **Paper abstract (read):** reasoning can be incentivised by pure reinforcement learning, without human-labelled reasoning, with emergent self-reflection, verification and strategy change, and the patterns can be used to improve smaller models (arXiv 2501.12948).
- **Observed side effect:** answer length keeps growing during RL training (lecture). The fixes called DAPO and Dr. GRPO are in [[GRPO]].
- **Controlling the thinking:** the lecture lists a dynamic token budget, context awareness, "budget forcing" (force it to keep thinking or to stop) and thoughts in a continuous latent space. I did not read those papers.
- **Release timeline (lecture slide):** OpenAI o1-preview on 12 September 2024, then DeepSeek's R1 on 20 January 2025, among others (the slide says it is illustrative and not complete).
- **Limits:** only works well where answers can be checked; costs more; the visible thinking is not guaranteed to be the real cause of the answer (from memory).

```python
import re
import numpy as np
# 1) the two rule-based rewards: is the thinking in its tags, and is the final answer right?
def fmt(txt): return bool(re.fullmatch(r'<think>.+?</think><answer>.+?</answer>', txt, re.S))
def acc(txt, truth):
    m = re.search(r'<answer>(.*?)</answer>', txt, re.S); return bool(m) and m.group(1).strip() == truth
cases = ['<think>2025-2020=5</think><answer>5</answer>', '<think>I guess</think><answer>4</answer>', 'The answer is 5', '<answer>5</answer>']
for c in cases: print(f'{c!r:52s} format {fmt(c)!s:5s} correct {acc(c, "5")}')
# 2) a toy of reinforcement learning with a checkable reward: three ways to answer a question, 16 tries per round
rng = np.random.RandomState(0)
p_right = np.array([0.30, 0.60, 0.85]); length = np.array([5, 60, 300])      # direct answer, short thinking, long thinking
z = np.zeros(3); softmax = lambda z: np.exp(z - z.max()) / np.exp(z - z.max()).sum()
for step in range(301):
    p = softmax(z)
    if step in (0, 100, 300): print(f'round {step:3d} | policy {p.round(3).tolist()} | expected answer length {float(p @ length):6.1f} tokens | expected accuracy {float(p @ p_right):.3f}')
    a = rng.choice(3, 16, p=p); r = (rng.rand(16) < p_right[a]).astype(float)
    adv = (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + 1e-8)                                 # group-relative advantage
    g = sum(adv[i] * (np.eye(3)[a[i]] - p) for i in range(16)) / 16
    z += 0.5 * g
```

## 🔴 4. Deep / formal
**The reward.** A common design adds a format check and an accuracy check:

$$
R=R_{\text{format}}+R_{\text{accuracy}},\qquad R_{\text{format}},R_{\text{accuracy}}\in\{0,1\}
$$

**Where:**
- $R_{\text{format}}$ is 1 if the output has the required structure (thinking in its tags, then the answer in its tags) and 0 if not.
- $R_{\text{accuracy}}$ is 1 if the extracted final answer matches the ground truth (or passes the tests), and 0 if not.
- $R$ is the total reward the attempt gets, which then goes into the group advantage of [[GRPO]].
- **Matters:** the format term makes the thinking *readable and parseable*, and the accuracy term is the real goal. Without the accuracy term nothing pushes the model toward correct thinking. Equal weights of 1 are my choice for the example; the lecture only says the reward is formatting plus accuracy.

**Example (tiny numbers):** the ground truth is "5".
- `<think>2025-2020=5</think><answer>5</answer>`: format 1, accuracy 1, so $R=\mathbf{2}$.
- `<think>I guess</think><answer>4</answer>`: format 1, accuracy 0, so $R=\mathbf{1}$.
- `<answer>5</answer>` (no thinking tags): format 0, accuracy 1 by the code's lenient answer check, so $R=\mathbf{1}$.
- `The answer is 5`: no tags at all, so $R=\mathbf{0}$.
- Meaning: only the first answer earns full reward, the other three are pushed down.

**Further notes:**
- **What the toy shows:** the code is a three-option choice (answer directly, think briefly, think at length) with made-up success chances $0.30$, $0.60$ and $0.85$ and lengths $5$, $60$ and $300$ tokens. Rewarding only correctness, the policy moved from equal odds ($0.583$ expected accuracy, $121.7$ tokens) to $0.978$ on long thinking after 100 rounds ($0.842$ accuracy, $294.2$ tokens) and $0.995$ after 300.
- **Do not over-read the toy:** the success chances and lengths are invented, there is no length cost, and nothing is being reasoned. It only shows why a correctness-only reward drifts toward longer thinking *when* longer thinking pays. The real length growth the lecture mentions has an additional cause linked to how averaging over tokens treats long wrong answers (see [[GRPO]]).
- **Not covered:** how the thinking text is generated inside the model, process rewards, and safety of hidden reasoning.

**What came before, and how this improved on it:**
- **Before:** [[Chain of Thought]] prompting (2022), which shows examples of reasoning in the prompt, and supervised training on human-written step-by-step solutions (the latter from the lecture's framing).
- **Its limit:** a person has to write the reasoning, which is slow, and the model is limited to human-style thinking.
- **What changed:** reinforcement learning from a checkable reward, so the model finds its own reasoning (DeepSeek-AI, arXiv 2501.12948, 22 January 2025, abstract read).
- **How much better:** the abstract says the trained model surpasses counterparts trained on human demonstrations on verifiable tasks (maths, coding competitions, STEM), but gives no numbers, and I did not read the results tables. In this note's toy, expected accuracy rose from $0.583$ to $0.848$ while the answer length rose from $121.7$ to $298.6$ tokens, so the gain came with a cost in tokens.
